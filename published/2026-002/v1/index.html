Abstract

This survey maps published work on shock testing with more than one shaker: how shock tests are specified, how one shaker is driven to meet a shock specification, how several shakers reproduce a waveform or meet a shock response spectrum (SRS) at several points, how the structures under test change with test level, and what learning-based methods offer. Reproducing a waveform with several shakers is well described for the first pulse. SRS control with several shakers is not: one patent application states its update law and was demonstrated at low levels, one journal paper could be read only as an abstract, controller makers rarely state their laws, and a published user report found that a commercial mode put about five times the specified peak acceleration on one axis. Measured frequency drops are about 0.1 to 4 % for well-tightened bolted joints at low force, about 6 to 10 % for a lightly torqued joint, a single bolt and a press-fit foam-and-metal interface, and about 28 % on one beam driven into full slip, with damping rising about 1.4 to 20 times; on one friction interface, shaker shocks did not move the frequency that sine sweeps lowered. No multi-shaker SRS law tested on such a structure, no standard or tested acceptance rule for several control points, no rule for the timing between them, and no learned component in a multi-shaker shock control loop was found in a search of open English-language databases, the open web and patents, with a few Chinese-language web and patent queries, in November 2026. Several key papers could be read only as abstracts.

Summary

A shock test puts a short, violent motion into a test article to show that it survives the shocks it will see in service. Most shock tests are run on one shaker, one axis at a time. Real shocks arrive in several directions at once, so test engineers have begun running shocks with several shakers driving the article together. This survey sets out what has been published on doing that.

Most shocks are specified by a shock response spectrum (SRS): the peak response of a row of simple oscillators driven by the shock. Many different waveforms share one SRS, so the standards also limit duration. None of the standards read gives a tolerance on the timing between control points, or a rule for accepting a shock judged at several control points with several drives; two that might could not be read.

With one shaker, a waveform is built to match the SRS and corrected pulse by pulse as the level rises, each correction kept smaller than the error because the structure changes with level. With several shakers, reproducing a waveform on the first pulse is well described. Correcting between pulses is described by controller makers in outline and in one 1979 report whose law was never run. The theory of repeated trials gives a plain condition, proved for one drive and a linear structure: the model’s phase error must stay under 90 degrees at every frequency. Meeting an SRS at several points with several shakers is thin in the literature.

Joints soften and gain damping as the level rises: by about 0.1 to 4 % in frequency when well tightened, and more when loose, frictional or slipping. The few measurements made at shock level disagree with hammer and sine tests. A model measured at low level describes the structure at low level. Learning-based methods have not yet reached a multi-shaker shock control loop in print.

How the Literature Was Searched and Read

Scope. Shock tests on electrodynamic or hydraulic shakers with more than one drive, the single-shaker methods they build on, the standards that specify them, measurements of structures whose response changes with test level, and learning-based methods that bear on any of these. Resonant plates and six-degree-of-freedom tables are included as the multi-axis alternatives to shaker shock. Seismic shaking tables and road simulators appear only as lineage.

Search. From 11 to 15 November 2026: OSTI (the U.S. Department of Energy’s Office of Scientific and Technical Information), Crossref, arXiv, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration’s (NASA’s) Technical Reports Server, Google Patents and the open web, in English, with partial Semantic Scholar coverage, and references followed forward and backward from the key papers. In Chinese: three web queries (27 results screened, on multi-shaker SRS control, multi-axis shock waveform replication, and shock control patents) and one patent query. Not searched: Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, the Chinese databases China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and Wanfang, the Defense Technical Information Center catalog (unavailable at the time), the full text of SAE International and Institute of Environmental Sciences and Technology (IEST) papers, and the Shock and Vibration Symposium archive. 1. How a Shock Test Is Specified

1.1 Classical Pulses

The oldest shock specification is a pulse of prescribed shape: a half-sine, a sawtooth or a trapezoid, of stated peak and duration, inside a tolerance band. International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) standard 60068-2-27 (Test Ea) “is written in terms of prescribed pulse shapes” (its scope; only its public page and preview were read). A shaker cannot end a test moving, so a classical pulse on a shaker needs small pulses before and after it that bring the table’s velocity and displacement back to zero, which changes its SRS at low frequencies. In the U.S. Department of Defense standard MIL-STD-810H with Change 1 (2022), Method 516.8, for functional shock, classical pulses “are unacceptable unless it can be demonstrated during tailoring that the field shock environment time trace approximates such a form” (4.6.2).

1.2 The Shock Response Spectrum

A shock response spectrum (SRS) describes a shock by its effect rather than its waveform. Imagine a row of simple oscillators, each a mass on a spring with a damper, each tuned to a different natural frequency, all fixed to the point where the acceleration was measured. Drive them with the measured acceleration and record the largest response of each. Plotted against the oscillators’ frequencies, those peaks are the SRS. The oscillators’ damping is given as a quality factor Q; Q = 10 is 5 % of critical damping. A maximax SRS takes the largest absolute response at any time, during or after the shock.

The SRS loses the waveform. Method 516.8 says it plainly: “There is no unique synthesized complex transient pulse satisfying a given SRS” (2.3.3.1). Cap and Smallwood (1997) open with the consequence: “it is commonly accepted that different agencies can generate vastly different time domain test inputs whose SRS all satisfy the test requirements within a stated tolerance band.” For an SRS with a hump at low frequency and a lower flat plateau above it, Heitman and Cap (2019) show that the plateau can be met “with little or no energy in that region”, because the high-frequency oscillators respond to the peak of the low-frequency motion.

Method 516.8 ranks how a shock should be run, best first: replication of a measured waveform; an SRS from measured data; an SRS without measured data; and a classical pulse (2.3.2). An SRS from data is computed with Q = 10 at one-twelfth-octave spacing or finer; when only one or two records exist, a margin of 3 dB or 6 dB is added, chosen for the conservatism wanted. The tolerance (4.2.2.2) is -1.5 to +3 dB on at least 90 % of the one-twelfth-octave points from 10 to 2,000 Hz, and -3 to +6 dB on the rest. Several measurements in one zone may be judged by their average within stated bounds, and the standard warns that “individual measurements may be substantially out of tolerance while the average is within tolerance.” Synthesis from an SRS “is applicable only for ‘simple shocks’” (note to 2.3.1.2.3), and splitting an SRS into a low-frequency and a high-frequency test “is unacceptable” (2.3.3.1).

1.3 Effective Duration

Because one SRS fits many waveforms, Method 516.8 also limits duration (Annex A 1.3). The duration Te runs from the first zero crossing above the noise floor to the perceived end of the shock; when unknown it defaults to 2.5 divided by the lowest frequency of interest. The effective duration TE is the time that holds all the peaks above A/CF, where A is the largest peak and CF is the largest crest factor found over short intervals of Te (about Te/10); CF is taken as 3 when TE is not given. A test must hold 0.8 TE to 1.2 TE. Without measured data, TE is 2 divided by a cutoff frequency, 45 Hz in most categories (Table 516.8-III).

1.4 Temporal Moments

Cap and Smallwood (1997) proposed describing a shock also by its temporal moments, over the whole band or in frequency bands: the energy E (the integral of the squared acceleration), the root-mean-square (RMS) duration D (the spread of that energy in time) and the root-energy amplitude R (the square root of E/D), which play the parts of velocity change, pulse width and amplitude of a classical pulse (their Appendix A, credited to Smallwood 1994, read only as an abstract). For a shaker shock the authors offered, as a possibility, ±3 dB on the SRS, ±1 dB on E and R in each band and ±10 % on D, and suggested the SRS tolerance might relax to ±6 dB once band moments are held. Their Table III shows why: three inputs that all met one launch shock’s SRS had energies of 45, 290 and 353 g²s against the field’s 275 g²s, the shortest 7.9 dB low.

1.5 Waveforms

A shock can also be specified as a waveform and replicated. Method 525.2 (time waveform replication, TWR, on one exciter) observes that the drive is compensated “by linear convolution of the exciter system impulse response function estimate with the reference time trace”, and that “The condition of system linearity is almost never satisfied so that the reference time trace is averaged over time through the linear convolution” (4.2.2 a). For a replicated shock it asks the SRS at 5 % damping to be within -6 to +3 dB, and the largest positive and negative peaks (no more than 10 % of all peaks) within ±20 % (4.2.2 c(2)). The method is not quite consistent with itself: 4.2.2 b says replication tolerances should be no broader than those of other methods, yet -6 dB is broader on the low side than Method 516.8’s -1.5 dB.

Method 527.2 covers multi-exciter tests (METs) in two procedures: time-domain references (an extension of TWR) and spectral references (an extension of random). There is no procedure for an SRS specified at several points; shock enters only as a segment of a replicated waveform. The method does not address “vendor-supplied software control strategy for a MET” (1.3 a), “generally does not provide specific test tolerance information” (1.3 e), and states that “at this point in TWR test philosophy, test tolerance specification is not well quantified” (Annex C 3). Annex C defines energy-weighted global errors over all channels, including a global SRS error, with no tolerance values.

1.6 Other Standards

IEC 60068-2-81:2003 (Test Ei) specifies tests with a synthesized SRS on a vibration generator with a computer-based control system. IEST-RP-DTE022 (2014), “Multi-Shaker Test and Control”, gives recommendations for vibration or shock tests with multiple exciters. Only their public pages were read. The North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) Allied Environmental Conditions and Test Publication AECTP-400, Method 421, “Multi-Exciter Vibration and Shock Testing”, read in its superseded 2006 edition, lists “SRS Shock Synthesis - Tailored using wavelets, damped sinusoids or both. Different input shocks on each exciter”, takes its shock tolerances from its single-exciter methods, and says nothing about how the shocks on each exciter should relate in time. Whether the current edition (2025) keeps the method was not verified.

1.7 Several Axes at Once

Sisemore, Babuska and Flores (2020) set out what happens when three single-axis shock specifications are run at once. Three equal shocks in phase on three axes add to a vector √3 times one of them, “a 4.78dB over-test in one direction that will not align to any of the component principal directions”; inverting one of them changes only the direction of that peak, not its size. If the specifications describe one event measured in three axes, running them together may be acceptable, and may be more appropriate; if they describe separate events, or the orientation is simply unknown, a simultaneous test over-tests in some directions and under-tests in others. Hopkins and Sisemore (2019) make the same point for a resonant plate: “if the three responses are in-phase, then the component may be subjected to a much higher shock excitation in some arbitrary direction than in any of the three principal directions.” In the other direction, Sisemore, Babuska and Booher (2016) found that each of the six single-axis pseudo-velocity SRS (the SRS in velocity units) of a six-degree-of-freedom shaker shock lay well below the single-axis failure prediction for their beams, and the beams failed.

What no standard gives. None of the standards read gives a tolerance on the timing or phase between control points in a shock test, and none gives an acceptance rule for a shock judged at several control points with several drives; two that might (IEST-RP-DTE022 and the current AECTP-400) could not be read. Method 516.8’s zone average judges several measurements of one zone in a single-exciter test, with its own warning.

2. Shock on One Shaker

2.1 Building a Waveform From an SRS

A shaker plays a waveform, so an SRS must first be turned into one, and the waveform must start and end with the table at rest. Two building blocks are used. A decaying sinusoid of frequency f and damping ratio ζ is a(t)=Ae−2πζftsin⁡(2πft)a(t) = A\, e^{-2\pi\zeta f t} \sin(2\pi f t). A wavelet of the kind used in Smallwood’s WAVSYN synthesis is a sine of frequency f that completes N half-cycles inside a half-sine window, with N odd (Cap and Smallwood 1997, Eq. 3, which gives no reason for the odd N). Summing decaying sinusoids “compensated for shaker velocity and displacement limitations” is the subject of Smallwood and Nord (1974); that paper could not be obtained, and what it contains is known here only from later papers.

Sandia National Laboratories’ description of the fitting is in Heitman, Babuska, Cap and Reid (2016). The user sets the frequencies, decay rates and signs of the components, and only their amplitudes are fitted. “The algorithm steps from low frequency to high frequency because the low frequencies can strongly affect the higher frequencies while the high frequencies only weakly affect the low frequencies.” For tone ii at frequency fif_i, the sensitivity sis_i is estimated by perturbing the amplitude once (the change in amplitude divided by the change in SRS at fif_i), and the amplitude is updated as

Ai←|Ai|+0.8si[SRSref(fi)−SRS(fi)]. A_i \leftarrow |A_i| + 0.8\, s_i \left[ \mathrm{SRS}_{\mathrm{ref}}(f_i) - \mathrm{SRS}(f_i) \right].

After each sweep, a compensating pulse makes the acceleration, velocity and displacement begin and end at zero, and the sweep is repeated, “typically done 1-2 times”. A controller maker’s manual states a plainer rule, each amplitude multiplied by the ratio of reference to synthesized SRS at its frequency, and says of it: “it cannot be assured that the SRS is controlled … However, this equation is used because there is no other good simple method” (IMV 2019, 7.3.4).

The choice of components matters beyond the SRS. With components above the shaker’s first resonance, the amplifier’s peak voltage, not the table’s acceleration, may decide whether a test can run; keeping the best of many random component sets cut the predicted peak voltage by about 40 % for about 10 % more peak acceleration (Cap, Heitman, Raymer and Hunt 2018). Sets meeting the same SRS gave responses in a three-mass model with errors from 0.6 to 4.8 dB RMS above 200 Hz (Heitman and Cap 2019).

2.2 Correcting the SRS at the Test

The SRS achieved on the article differs from the one synthesized, because the drive passes through the shaker, the fixture and the article. Coleman, Smallwood and Skousen (2016) describe how Sandia’s controller TSHAKER corrected it. A frequency response function (FRF), the ratio of response to drive at each frequency, is measured from a low-level broadband pulse, and the drive for the waveform is computed from it. After each pulse, the error at each frequency is the achieved SRS divided by the reference SRS; limit channels count only where their error exceeds one; and the largest error at each frequency sets the correction. “The drive waveform is modified by updating the magnitude of the estimated FRF and not changing the phase.” The correction is weighted, and “The weighting for the correction is always less the 100% because of the non-linarites” [sic], the reasons given being that “Damping often increases with level” and that “Low frequency response will change the SRS at higher frequencies.” The level rises “typically in 3dB increments”, and the weights in use run from 50 % at -12 dB to 80 to 90 % at full level (their Table 1). The paper gives no equation; “an experienced operator is required to rationally pick these parameters.”

Anaya and Babuska (2024, slides) report that TSHAKER has been replaced by scripts that step up from about -12 dB and scale each pulse to full level to predict whether limits will be exceeded. Transmissibilities from low-level random “Tend to over-estimate responses, thus over-limiting” and “Do not capture non-linear behavior”; transmissibilities computed from the step-up shocks themselves “produce better predictions as dB level increases” but lag “one level behind”. Babuska and Cap (2023) call shaker shocks “run open loop” and “pre-planned”, in the sense that each pulse is computed before it is fired.

2.3 Correcting a Waveform on One Shaker

Underwood’s patent US 5,517,426 (first filed 1992, issued 1996) controls a waveform, not an SRS, on one exciter with one control channel, between pulses. It names the problem with a fixed, pre-measured frequency response: “non-linearities in the system’s response (the variation of the transfer function with the amplitude of the drive signal) and time variations in the system dynamics are not accounted for. This can produce situations where the drive signal fails to converge”. Its answer is to keep learning the model from the test pulses. With D the drive, C the measured response and R the target in the frequency domain, after pulse i:

Hi=(1−σ)Hi−1+σCiDi−1,Di=Di−1+αγiR−CiHi, H_i = (1-\sigma)\, H_{i-1} + \sigma \frac{C_i}{D_{i-1}}, \qquad D_i = D_{i-1} + \alpha\, \gamma_i \frac{R - C_i}{H_i},

with σ “usually chosen to be equal to 0.125” and α “typically … 0.75”. Here γi\gamma_i is a confidence between 0 and 1 built from how well the model predicted the last pulse (a clipped ratio, not a statistical coherence), and every division is guarded against noise. The first pulses are fired at low amplitude, raised “as greater confidence is gained”, and the test aborts if the error’s peak grows. The patent adds that “if the transfer function is known to be highly non-linear, i.e., its value changes drastically from cycle to cycle, then [σ] should be set equal to 1”. It gives no convergence proof and no measured results.

Karshenas, Dunnigan and Williams (2000), read as an abstract, control a shock pulse on one electrodynamic actuator with an adaptive inverse filter, and report convergence to the reference pulse.

3. Several Shakers Reproducing a Waveform

3.1 Lineage

Driving several actuators together to reproduce several responses is called multiple-input, multiple-output (MIMO) control. The earliest work found dates from the 1970s. Cryer, Nawrocki and Lund (1976), read as an abstract, describe a road simulator for heavy trucks that finds the road inputs from measured vehicle response “through a multiple input-multiple output systems analysis of the vehicle”; the abstract says nothing about iteration. Fisher (1977) describes a four-actuator electrohydraulic shaker of 0.62 MN, working from zero frequency to 500 Hz under direct digital control. Cross-coupling between actuators “has been a severe problem in multiple-actuator systems”, and the cure is “a compensation matrix, which is simply the product of the desired system-transfer matrix and the inverse of the measured transfer matrix”, applied at many frequencies, with the matrix measured “by perturbation techniques at the full test level to minimize nonlinear effects”. A transient program is listed for shock spectrum and seismic tests, but the report does not say how transients were built or whether they were corrected between pulses.

Rader and Berry (1979), for NASA, drove two electrodynamic shakers on a truss to produce “two independent transient waveforms to be applied simultaneously”. A rapid sine sweep fed to each shaker in turn gave the 2 by 2 transfer matrix; it was inverted at each frequency, multiplied by the target spectra and transformed back to time. Wave shapes came out right and amplitudes did not, with a system whose memory allowed only 128 frequency lines over 50 Hz. They devised an iteration but could not run it for lack of memory. With E the error spectrum, desired minus actual, it reads

Xnew=Xold+[TF]−1E, X_{\mathrm{new}} = X_{\mathrm{old}} + [\mathrm{TF}]^{-1} E,

with [TF] the transfer matrix: a correction through its fixed inverse at unit gain. Their Eq. 12 prints a minus sign, which with E so defined would move the drive away from the target; the form above is the one that converges. They added that “not every analytically generated desired response is necessarily attainable.”

Seat and durability rigs (De Cuyper and Verhaegen 2002), control with more control accelerometers than exciters (Underwood and Keller 2004) and hydraulic seismic tables (Shen et al. 2018; Guan, Xu, Xiong and Wang 2020), all known here from abstracts, use related inverse-model methods. Underwood’s sine patent (US 5,299,459, 1994) records that a fixed-gain multi-exciter correction needs a negative gain “when phase errors greater than 90° are present”; it is steady sine only, as is Liu, Cai, Yu and Yang (2017), whose printed drive update carries the same sign slip as Rader and Berry’s.

In shock specifically, Xu, Chen and You (2010), read as an abstract, computed the inputs of two shakers by a time-frequency transform and ran a shock on a cantilever beam within the tolerances of the reference signals. Deng, Yan and Niu (2017) replicated a classical pulse with three exciters (Section 4.4).

3.2 The Open-Loop Solve

At each frequency f, the responses at the control points Y(f) follow from the drives X(f) through the matrix of FRFs H(f), measured beforehand: Y = H X. The drives that best reproduce a target YtY_t, in the least-squares sense, are

X(f)=H+(f)Yt(f), X(f) = H^{+}(f)\, Y_t(f),

where H⁺ is the pseudo-inverse: the ordinary inverse when H is square and well conditioned, and otherwise the least-squares solution, usually computed through the singular value decomposition with the weakest directions limited (regularized). The drives are then transformed back to time.

Schultz (2019) reports Sandia’s first shock test with several shakers: two shakers on an aluminum plate with twelve accelerometers, the target being the plate’s response to a hammer blow. Without zero padding, the frequency-domain solve wraps the end of the record onto its start (circular convolution); padding the target with zeros removed those errors, and error remained near the onset of the shock. In simulation, with forces standing in for shakers, more inputs helped mainly near the onset, and a search over 153 pairs of locations found one pair that “allowed for very accurate replication of the target response”; the worst pair lay on the plate’s centerline and could not move certain modes. Schultz adds that “Several commercial control systems allow for multiple-input shock control however this test technique is still niche”, and that “there is too much response information to condense down into a single, scalar value to use as a pass/fail metric.”

Schultz and Carter (2024) set the solve out in seven steps for records longer than the measured FRFs can resolve: split the target into overlapping frames, zero pad each, transform, solve, transform back, and add the drive frames with windows that sum to one. They advise band-limiting and high-pass filtering the target, a sample rate “higher than just twice the maximum frequency of interest”, and regularization, which is “typically necessary to minimize the effects of noise”. In a simulation where the structure’s modes moved by up to about 6 % after the FRFs were measured, the time-response assurance criterion (TRAC, a normalized dot product of two time histories, 1 for the same shape and 0 for no likeness; Schultz 2019) fell to 0.96, 0.89 and 0.97, with under-tests at two outputs and a slight over-test at the third. They warn that TRAC “will be sensitive to any phase shift or frequency errors”.

3.3 Pre-Echo and Errors in the Measured Response

Coletti, Carter and Schultz (2025) show that noise in the measured FRFs puts drive energy before the first event: “if a shock event occurs at 2 seconds, Fourier deconvolution may yield high input levels at 1.9 seconds, resulting in a ‘pre-echo’ in the output waveform, wherein the system responds to the specification before it occurs.” They compare four repairs: tapering the tail of the impulse response, padding and truncating the response, enforcing causality with a Hilbert transform, and fitting the measured responses with 1,200 single-mode curves under a minimum-energy penalty. In a linear simulation of 3 inputs and 8 outputs the curve fit was best “by a significant margin”, which the authors call unsurprising in a linear simulation, and above about 30 dB signal-to-noise ratio the taper and the Hilbert repair made the error worse. They state that “results may change if there are systematic FRF errors (such as those from leakage) or if the system is nonlinear.”

3.4 Correcting Between Pulses

Repeating a test and correcting the drive from the last error is called iterative learning control in the control literature: a system repeats one task from the same starting state, and after each trial the drive is updated from the error so that the error falls from trial to trial (Owens, Hätönen and Daley 2006). The simplest form corrects through an inverse model Ĝ of the article and shaker, with a learning gain β between 0 and 1:

uk+1=uk+βĜ−1ek,ek=r−yk. u_{k+1} = u_k + \beta\, \hat{G}^{-1} e_k, \qquad e_k = r - y_k .

Rader and Berry’s iteration is this law with β = 1; Underwood’s is close to it with β = 0.75 and an updated model. Let U be the ratio of the true frequency response to the model’s at each frequency. The error then falls on every trial, from any starting error, if

|1β−U(ejω)|<1βat every frequency, that is|1−βU(ejω)|<1 \left| \frac{1}{\beta} - U(e^{j\omega}) \right| < \frac{1}{\beta} \quad \text{at every frequency, that is} \quad \left| 1 - \beta U(e^{j\omega}) \right| < 1

(Harte, Hätönen and Owens 2005, as quoted in Owens, Hätönen and Daley 2006; the second form follows by multiplying through by β). In plain words: the model may be wrong in phase by anything less than 90 degrees at any frequency, and the larger its error, the smaller the gain must be (Harte 2007, Remark 5.3). Model errors at high frequency, such as resonances the model lacks, force a small gain and slow learning. These results are for one input and one output and for linear, unchanging systems, and the authors name several loops and nonlinearity as future work (journal version: Owens, Hätönen and Daley 2009). No source read in the shock literature states a convergence condition for correcting several drives between pulses. A survey of iterative learning control (Bristow, Tharayil and Alleyne 2006) was read only as an abstract. Method 527.2 adds a limit that applies to any update of a multi-drive model: “for any frequencies for which the drive signals are fully correlated, corrections to the system transfer function will not be possible” (Annex B 3.5, written for random control).

3.5 Identification

Every method above needs the matrix H measured first. Method 527.2 says the first drives “must be statistically independent, and form vectors that are linearly independent with respect to the DOFs [degrees of freedom] to be tested” (4.5.2 a, Step 3). Published practice uses uncorrelated random noise or bursts on all drives at once (Spectral Dynamics 2007; Krieghbaum and Danforth 2026; the patent CN115130506A), random noise (Deng, Yan and Niu 2017), or a sine sweep on each shaker in turn (Rader and Berry 1979). The level at which H is measured matters, as Section 5 shows: Fisher measured it at full test level, Underwood kept estimating it from the test pulses, and Method 527.2 Annex D 3 (written for random) says controllers measure it at a very low level and may update it, because “The updated transfer function accounts for certain vibration system amplitude nonlinearities”.

4. Several Shakers and an SRS

4.1 One Drive, an SRS at Several Points

The single-drive methods of Section 2 were extended at Sandia to several response points. Heitman, Babuska, Cap and Reid (2016) compute each point’s response from the drive through measured FRFs and judge the match by each point’s SRS error normalized by its own reference, averaged with weights μj\mu_j chosen by the user. The amplitude update, written in the sign of their single-point Eq. 4, is

Ai←Ai+csi∑jμj[SRSref,j(fi)−SRSj(fi)]∑jμj, A_i \leftarrow A_i + c\, s_i\, \frac{\sum_j \mu_j \left[\mathrm{SRS}_{\mathrm{ref},j}(f_i) - \mathrm{SRS}_j(f_i)\right]}{\sum_j \mu_j},

with sis_i the change of the tone’s amplitude per change of the weighted SRS, from one perturbation, and c a constant the paper does not define. Their Eq. 8 prints the difference the other way round, which with c positive would move the amplitude away from the target; this is taken here as a printing slip. On a model of springs and masses, the drive so derived gave responses “considerably closer” to the targets than a drive fitted to the input SRS alone. The authors note that with one input a perfect match at several points is typically not possible, and suggest a weighting that limits response to prevent an over-test at chosen points. Babuska and Cap (2023, slides) describe the current form: the same tone-by-tone fitting with a weighted sum of per-location errors, random choice of component frequencies with the best set kept, and response limiting by the largest exceedance over limit channels, for which “Convergence is not guaranteed”; on a test article, “The responses were not as accurate as expected”. Cap, Heitman, Raymer and Hunt (2018), despite “Single Input Multiple Output” in their title, fit one SRS at the control point (Section 2.1). Ross, Schneider and Kalinich (2026) derived a single-axis table input from four gauges on a bolted joint by inverting measured transmissibilities and specified the table by the SRS of the result; they assumed linearity, and attribute their largest discrepancy, a mode shift near 170 Hz, possibly to nonlinear behavior.

4.2 Several Drives, an SRS at Several Points

The only published methods found for controlling an SRS at several points with several drives are two items from one group at the Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics.

Zheng, Ma, Wei and Chen (2023, online 2022) could be read only as an abstract. It describes building a reference waveform from an SRS by “random delay superposition” (component delays drawn from a normal distribution) or by filtering measured shocks; drives from “A time-domain inverse system method … based on a finite-difference equation”; and “A closed-loop iteration control procedure … to correct the drive signals according to the deviations between the responses and corresponding reference values”, shown in a simulation and a triaxial shaker test. The correction law, its gain and any convergence argument are not in the abstract. The random delays shape one waveform’s decay; they are not a rule for timing between control points.

The patent application CN115130506A (Zheng, Chen and Wei 2022), filed 25 days after the paper went online and deemed withdrawn in 2025, was read in full, its equations from the published images. For a triaxial shaker with three control channels, it builds each reference waveform r(t) from filtered copies of a measured field shock, computes the drive as x(t)=H−1r(t)x(t) = H^{-1} r(t) from a frequency response H measured once with uncorrelated white noise, and after each pulse corrects the target:

Sc(k+1)=EβSc(k),E=SrSy−1,Sc(1)=Sr, S_c^{(k+1)} = E^{\beta}\, S_c^{(k)}, \qquad E = S_r\, S_y^{-1}, \qquad S_c^{(1)} = S_r ,

where SrS_r holds the reference SRS, SyS_y the achieved SRS and ScS_c the corrected target from which the next reference waveform is synthesized, and β is called the convergence factor. The patent does not say whether the inverse and the power act element by element; with one SRS value per channel and frequency, the element-wise reading (each channel’s target scaled by its own ratio of reference to achieved SRS, raised to β) is the only one that makes sense, and it is an inference. In that reading the law is the multi-drive counterpart of Coleman, Smallwood and Skousen’s single-drive magnitude correction, moved from the FRF to the target, with no update of H described. The demonstration held all three axes within ±3 dB from 10 to 2,000 Hz at reference SRS levels of about 0.01 to 1 g (read off a logarithmic plot), far below qualification levels. Nothing is said about level steps, nonlinearity, timing between axes, drive limits, the value of β or convergence.

4.3 Shock on Random Vibration

A shock on top of random vibration adds a second problem: separating the two in the measured response. Zou, Li and Wang (2019), of the China Aerospace Science and Industry Corporation, ran an aluminum cylinder on five exciters, three for random vibration and two for shock in a perpendicular plane, with two commercial controllers in parallel, because MIMO vibration and MIMO shock follow different control principles and need separate controllers. A real-time separator removed from the shock control signals the parts coherent with the vibration, so the shock controller controlled only the shock-excited response. Three consecutive 10 g, 20 ms half-sines on 4.2 g RMS random met tolerance. The authors note that when vibration and shock act in the same direction only waveform replication can do it, and that separation degrades when nonlinearity lowers coherence.

The Nanjing group’s work is known here from abstracts and a patent. Ma, Chen, Lu and Zheng (2023, online 2022), read as an abstract, extract a half-sine from the mixed signal with an empirical wavelet transform (a way of splitting a signal into bands fitted to its own spectrum) and correct the shock amplitude and the random spectra separately. Of Zheng, Ren, Chen and Chen (2021) only the record could be obtained, and nothing of its content is cited. The patent CN113155385B (Zheng and Chen 2022), read in part in machine translation, builds the random part from a factor of the reference spectral matrix with random phases and the shock from a half-sine with two compensating pulses; after each frame it finds the shock’s amplitude and position as those that minimize the kurtosis (peakedness) of what is left, and corrects the two parts separately, L←EsLL \leftarrow E_s L for the random part’s factor and A←EkAA \leftarrow E_k A for the shock amplitude. The definitions of EsE_s and EkE_k are in equation images that were not opened.

Cross, Melendez, Arnold, Brenner, Throneberry and Soine (2025) combined a gas-gun pyroshock on a resonant plate with 12-shaker MIMO random vibration. The shock was not controlled: the gun pressure was stepped and the gun fired by hand. The shock clipped the random control channels, and in the test shown random control was lost after the pulse.

4.4 Three Exciters and a Classical Pulse

Deng, Yan and Niu (2017), of the China Academy of Engineering Physics, replicated a classical pulse in three axes with three exciters, one per axis. The matrix H was measured with broadband random, and the drive was computed from its inverse and corrected from the deviation between the measured and target responses; the correction law is not stated. After several corrections a 10 g, 5 ms half-sine on all three axes met the Chinese military tolerance on the main pulse, with local exceedances in the compensating segments before and after it.

4.5 Six-Degree-of-Freedom Tables and Resonant Plates

Sandia has run shocks on a six-degree-of-freedom shaker table. Sisemore, Babuska and Booher (2016, 2017) applied 3,424 shocks “simultaneously in all six degrees of freedom” to 68 cantilever beams; how the six-axis drive was synthesized or controlled is not described. Owens, Tipton and McDowell (2015) ran six-degree-of-freedom random tests on a commercial table and treated shock by analysis only.

Resonant plates make several axes with no drive control at all. Hopkins and Sisemore (2019) moved the projectile impact off the center of a plate to excite three axes in one hit, at the cost of about 50 % more impulse, and met three single-axis specifications at once (average errors -0.8, +2.6 and +4.8 dB). Sisemore, Babuska and Flores (2020) answer that off-center tests “are actually not three-axis shock tests, but rather single axis shocks at an arbitrary angle”. Bouma, Schoenherr and Soine (2023) design a fixture for the purpose by simulation, noting that multi-axis shock tests “are not always repeatable and greatly depend on the fixture”; Alvis and Mesh (2025, slides) optimize the plate’s geometry.

4.6 A User’s Report of a Commercial Multi-Shaker SRS Mode

Krieghbaum and Danforth (2026), of Los Alamos National Laboratory, attempted a three-axis flight shock with one large axial shaker and two small modal shakers on stingers, three drives and one triaxial control point. The commercial MIMO shock mode they used would not accept a waveform for each control axis: “the user must specify the SRS, then the software will generate an arbitrary time waveform.” The result was an over-test, with a peak Y acceleration of 16.1 g against a specified 3.08 g. “Unfortunately, the software is proprietary, so we are unable to explore what strategy the controller was using to create this shock pulse.” They conclude that “the SRS alone does not provide enough information about control performance and data quality”, and that what alarm and abort percentages are acceptable in multi-shaker tests has had “no investigation”.

4.7 What Controller Makers Publish

MIL-STD-810H Method 527.2 Annex A records that “As of the inclusion of this new test method into MIL-STD-810G, the primary vibration control system vendors offer MET options for time waveform replication (TWR), sine, shock, and random”; that statement dates from 2008. The public pages read on 15 November 2026 describe multi-shaker shock or SRS modes (Crystal Instruments, with Zhuge 2024; Data Physics; Siemens, Musella 2023; Vibration Research) but state no correction law. Siemens synthesizes sets of shocks to meet “a specific max cross-correlation requirement and maximum SRS error”. The two that state the most are in Table 1: a 2007 Spectral Dynamics data sheet gives equalization by “Inverse Frequency Response Matrix”, gains from 0.05 to 1, level steps of 0.1 to 10 dB, and output one level step above full level “for difficult nonlinear test conditions”; IMV’s 2019 manual runs an SRS shock as waveform control of a synthesized reference. The pages that could be read from two further makers (their own pages were blocked) described no multi-shaker SRS mode.

4.8 The Open-Source Controller Rattlesnake

Rattlesnake, Sandia’s open-source controller, has a MIMO transient environment, documented in Rohe, Schultz and Hunter (2022) and in its user’s manual (2024), whose prose was read; its source code was not. It takes a set of time histories as its specification. Its FRFs come from a system identification before the test, and “During control, the environment will not attempt to update the transfer function relationships, though control can still be updated using the previously acquired responses to the signal.” The user supplies the control law; the manual’s worked examples load a pseudo-inverse law and start at -12 dB “so we don’t break anything”, and on modal shakers “the initial impulse cannot be achieved as well with the modal shakers as it was with the impact hammer.” The documentation describes no SRS control.

5. Structures That Change With Level

5.1 Terms

Every model in Sections 2 to 4 is a set of FRFs measured before the test. A structure whose FRFs depend on how hard it is driven is said here to change with level. Bolted joints are the usual cause. Under small loads the contact surfaces stick everywhere but at their edges, where they slip a little (microslip); the joint loses a little stiffness and dissipates energy, so the resonance moves down (softening) and the damping ratio (the fraction of critical damping) rises. Under large loads the whole interface slides (macroslip). Thin plates and panels change with level for reasons of geometry: as they deflect, their midplanes stretch and they stiffen (hardening), or a curved panel first softens. A curve of a mode’s frequency or damping against its amplitude is called its backbone.

Well-tightened bolted joints at low force soften about 0.1 to 4 %. Mayes, Pacini and Roettgen (2016) put the picture in a sentence: “Many structural dynamic systems are mildly nonlinear in stiffness (few percent modal frequency change) and significantly nonlinear in damping (hundreds of percent damping ratio change) as a function of amplitude”. None of these tests reached macroslip, so “one cannot know whether the frequency would shift further if even larger forces were applied” (Roettgen 2016, p. 68). A joint’s stiffness loss stays hidden because “the elastic subsystem is generally much more compliant than the interface until the interface has been forced into the vicinity of macro-slip” (Segalman 2002).

Looser and friction joints soften more. The two-beam structure’s abstract calls all its levels “below macro-slip”, while its Section 6 suggests macroslip began at the highest (Deaner et al. 2015). The press-fit slug is a friction interface, not a bolt, and part of its shift may be preload lost during testing, which averaged 162 to 352 lbf (Jacobs-O’Malley and Hofer 2017). The single bolt’s figures come from slides, and its damping turned down above about 5 lbf RMS, which other sources read as the onset of macroslip (Clark, Allen and Pacini 2023; Wall, Allen and Kuether 2022). The 28 % is one beam hammered into “definite macroslip”; afterwards it stayed about 11 % low (Bonney et al. 2016).

Damping rises about 1.4 to 20 times, and sometimes falls: on the single bolt, and on one S4 beam mode whose damping halved as its frequency rose about 2 %. That mode’s authors could not identify the cause: “the nonlinear response is dominated by some unknown mechanism other than microslip” (Wall, Allen and Kuether 2022).

Other hardening, and softening then hardening, come from geometry. A cantilever stretching a clamped thin strip (a purpose-built benchmark), thin flat plates, and a clamped cross beam after an initial fall that its authors attribute to gravity sag and imperfect clamping all harden; curved panels soften first. Amabili (2018), read as an abstract, puts geometric frequency shifts at commonly 10 to 25 % at most for shells, plates and beams. For a bolted curved panel, its authors judge that “the effect of the frictional clamping on the amplitude-dependent modal properties is rather weak, whereas a pronounced frequency variation is evident from the geometric nonlinearity” (Bhattu et al. 2024); models of it with friction alone predicted only 2 to 4 % softening, and the contact “has a crucial effect on the geometrically nonlinear behavior” (Krack et al. 2025).

One mode can depend on another. On the S4 beam the damping of mode 2 depended on how strongly mode 1 was excited, and not the reverse (Wall, Allen and Kuether 2022). A loosely bolted tester showed modes coupled through an internal resonance (Cenedese et al. 2022), and with two modes 0.5 Hz apart a single shaker could not hold a beam on one nonlinear mode (Renson et al. 2016).

Structures scatter and change during a test. Preload for a given torque varied by up to 70 to 100 % between assemblies of one beam (Wall, Allen and Kuether 2022); C-beams cracked by fatigue between runs (Davis et al. 2024). An industrial structure’s low-level frequency and damping moved about 7 % and 20 % over its test series (Pacini 2025): “the underlying linear properties can change throughout the test series.” A review of joint experiments reports that “the uncertainty measured is often greater than the nonlinear characteristics being studied” (Brake, Schwingshackl and Reuß 2019, read as an abstract).

Shock is not sine. Jacobs-O’Malley and Hofer (2017) tested the same kind of friction interface both ways. Sine sweeps from 1 to 10 g lowered its frequency about 8 to 10 % (Kuether and Najera 2017, read off a figure); shaker shock pulses from 8.5 to 35 g did not change it: “This result suggests that when the excitation is transient in nature, the system stiffness does not change with amplitude as it does with the harmonic excitation.” The authors offer as a possible explanation that the dominant nonlinearity depends on the type of excitation. Different foam cups were used for the shock tests, and the section’s damping results contradict its text, so only the frequency result is cited.

At pyroshock level. Schoenherr, Soine and Witt (2022) fitted modal parameters to a resonant plate during projectile shocks. Against a hammer test about two orders of magnitude lower in force, five modes moved down 1.6 to 12.7 % and one rose 20 %, its damping from 1.4 % to 9.6 %; between low-level and high-level shocks the changes were small. The plate’s damping bars are bolted loosely over rubber, and the shock responses have about 24 Hz line spacing, which limits precision. The authors write that “predicting the shifts in frequency and damping is very difficult or impossible without data”, and that the nonlinearities “do not prevent the use of a linearized model that is calibrated to the dynamics present at shock type input loads.” A later paper on the same plate found its 582 Hz mode no longer proportional to hammer force above about 7,500 N (Soine 2024).

5.3 What This Means for a Test Controlled From a Low-Level Model

Identification theory states the general point. The best linear approximation (BLA) of a nonlinear system is the linear model that best fits its output, in the mean-square sense, for a given class of inputs; what it cannot fit behaves like noise. Schoukens, Vaes and Pintelon (2016) show that it depends on the excitation: “Be aware that the BLA varies in general as a function of the power spectrum and amplitude distribution of the excitation signal. For that reason, the excitation signals during the experiments should match as well as possible the signals that will be applied later on to the model”. A linear model fitted at one level “fails completely” above it. An FRF measured with low-level random noise is the BLA for that level and that kind of signal, not for a full-level shock pulse.

Measured consequences are of the order of a factor of two. A linear model over-predicted some modes of a jointed structure at high level by a factor of 2 (Mayes, Pacini and Roettgen 2016, slides), and linear models of two industrial structures over-predicted their peak responses by up to 2 and by 1.5 to 1.8 (Pacini 2025). Clark, Allen and Pacini (2023) go further with a single-mode joint model fitted to measured damping and extrapolated past the measured data into macroslip, with the frequency held fixed. In that model, raising the environment a thousand times in microslip raised the response only 53 times, while near macroslip doubling the force raised it more than 6,000 times; their slides conclude that “A small increase in the shaker power will increase the response greatly” and “may lead to difficulties in control”. That hazard is a result of the model extrapolated beyond the data, not a measured control event. Their paper warns that a model made at low level and extrapolated up “can be highly over conservative of [sic] highly under conservative depending on whether the joints are exercised above or below macro slip”.

Practice points the same way: corrections weighted below 100 % because of nonlinearity, predictions from the step-up shocks rather than low-level random (Section 2.2), and models learned from the test pulses or measured at full level (Sections 2.3 and 3.1). Lacayo et al. (2019) abandoned constant-force stepped sine above 2 N because the force could not be held constant near resonance. Larsen (2018), whose thesis was read only as an abstract, reports a method “to estimate high amplitude shock inputs from low amplitude frequency response functions”. No published account was found of a multi-shaker shock controller run on a structure measured to change with level.

6. Learning-Based Approaches

Here learning-based means a model whose form is fitted to data, usually a neural network (a flexible function with many adjustable weights), rather than derived from physics.

Shaker drives. Peláez-Rodríguez, Magdaleno, Salcedo-Sanz and Lorenzana (2023) and the extended paper of 2024 drive one inertial electrodynamic actuator to reproduce human bouncing and walking forces of 1 to 2.5 Hz. The network is trained entirely in simulation through a fitted linear model of the actuator; the reported errors (53.26 N and 62.72 N RMS on average) are simulated, and no classical method was run beside it. Larbi et al. (2020) placed a small network in the acceleration feedback path of a small electric shaking table beside its position controller; on earthquake records the time-domain error fell from 54 to 67 % to 13 to 18 %, against the table’s own displacement controller as the only baseline, and the peak error on one record got worse (2.56 % to 10.56 %). Najera-Flores (2019, slides) learned the map from measured responses to the base input of a six-degree-of-freedom shaker from ground-test data, which “could be white noise”.

From an SRS to a waveform. Watts, Jeon, Newton and Bowering (2026a, arXiv, not peer reviewed), of Los Alamos National Laboratory with the University of Rochester, train a conditional variational autoencoder (a network that learns to generate examples matched to a given input) on 400,000 synthetic and 99,682 measured shocks to map one SRS to one acceleration record. On four held-out sets its mean logarithmic SRS error was lower than a classical decaying-sinusoid fit (0.095 against 0.206 on one set), in about 0.3 ms against 5 to 8 s. Nothing in the method constrains shaker displacement, velocity or force, and a single reconstruction “may not be feasible for a given shaker table”. The follow-on (Watts et al. 2026b, arXiv) trains 104 models to give several candidate waveforms, because single models gave “nearly identical waveforms” under sampling, and found “Spectral agreement alone does not establish physical suitability”. A 2026 report (Watts 2026, slides) moves to a diffusion model (a network that builds a sample by removing noise step by step), whose baseline “does not consistently meet the target SRS tolerance” without a refinement step. All three are one channel and open loop, with no shaker.

Inverse models with physics. Bolderman et al. (2024) build a feedforward controller for an industrial linear motor from a physical model plus a small network kept near the physics where there is no data; it cut the mean tracking error about two times against the physical model alone. They state the risk plainly: networks “suffer from bad extrapolation outside the training data set, which makes them unsafe for implementation in industry”.

Further away from a shaker. A transformer (the network type behind large language models) trained on many simulated systems of one class predicts a new system’s output without retraining; when the class was widened at test time its simulation error rose from 0.013 to 0.066, about five times (Forgione, Pura and Piga 2023, who set 0.066 against their one-step-ahead figure, 0.009). Networks fitted to jointed structures serve as surrogates of physics models (Najera-Flores et al. 2024) or are fitted to one measured decay and tested on its later, lower-amplitude part (Sharma et al. 2024), or learn a correction to a physical model with displacement and velocity measured everywhere (Lai et al. 2021); none was tested above the level of its data.

What was not found. In the searches described above, which for this topic were in English only, no learned component was found inside a multi-shaker shock or SRS control loop, open or closed. No learned shaker drive law on hardware was found compared with offline waveform replication or iterative learning control, and none was tested above the level of its training data on a structure that changes with level.

No multi-shaker SRS law tested on a structure that changes with level. The one readable law (CN115130506A) identifies its model once, describes no update of it, and was shown at SRS levels of about 0.01 to 1 g. The one journal paper (Zheng et al. 2023) could be read only as an abstract. The one published user report (Krieghbaum and Danforth 2026) found an over-test from a law the users could not see. Single-shaker practice deals with level by weighting corrections below 100 % and re-estimating from the step-up shocks; no multi-shaker counterpart has been published. A companion article in preparation reports simulation work on this problem.

No multi-drive SRS sensitivity step. Sandia’s published practice, as of 2023, adjusts each component’s amplitude of one drive through a single scalar sensitivity, before the pulse (Heitman et al. 2016; Babuska and Cap 2023). No source was found that corrects component amplitudes through the sensitivity of each SRS point to each of several drives, or that uses the derivative of the maximax SRS with respect to the drives. The nearest are quasi-Newton waveform methods on hydraulic tables (methods that improve their own estimate of the sensitivities from each trial), known from abstracts.

No rule for timing between control points. None of the standards read gives one. The nearest published reasoning is about polarity and in-phase combination (Section 1.7); one maker synthesizes sets of shocks to a cross-correlation limit (Musella 2023).

No standard or tested acceptance rule for several control points. Method 527.2 defines global errors without values. A user report asks for alarm and abort limits that “there has been no investigation” into, and argues that the SRS alone is not enough; Schultz (2019) finds no single scalar that will do; per-axis SRS can understate a simultaneous shock (Sisemore, Babuska and Booher 2016). Cross and Zwink (2026) proposed a practice for judging multi-shaker tests, including shock: keep some accelerometers out of the control and judge with them, report what the setup could reach, what the controller achieved and what the article saw, and judge a controller first by its safety. In their simulations, twenty synthesized pulses matching one multi-point SRS target about equally differed by about 1.5 dB (one standard deviation) in element peak stress, and a zone-average rule sorted the pulses little better than chance. That is a proposal resting on simulation, not a standard.

Few measurements of joints under shock. The shock-level measurements found are the Ministack ring-downs under shaker shock and the resonant plate at pyroshock level. No measurement was found of one jointed structure under shaker shock at several qualification levels, with an identification at low level compared against the full-level response.

No convergence statement for correcting several drives between shocks. The conditions available are general results of iterative learning control for one input and one output.

Leads not followed. Every reference states how much of it was read. Favour’s 1974 review of transient waveform control (cited in Underwood’s shock patent), papers by Spectral Dynamics engineers at the 2008 and 2010 Shock and Vibration Symposia on multi-shaker waveform replication, and Larsen et al. (2020) on judging multi-axis SRS results, of which only the record was found, were not read.
