Abstract

A multi-shaker random vibration test drives several shakers at once so that a set of accelerometers on the test article follow target spectra and, for every pair of them, a target coherence and phase. The rule that turns the targets and a measurement of the system into shaker drives is the control law. Hertzforge, an open-source structural dynamics suite, ships six: the pseudo-inverse with a closed-loop correction of a factor of the target (the default), the same pseudo-inverse without feedback, a shape-constrained law that drives only the directions the system responds to most, a law that takes its cross terms from the article’s own response to uncorrelated drive, a law that chooses the cross terms needing the least drive, and Brandon Zwink’s band-average law, which matches averages over fractional-octave bands rather than single frequency lines. This article explains in plain terms what a multi-shaker controller does, and for each law states what it does, the problem it solves as an equation, the derivation, the algorithm step by step with the file and function that does each step, every setting with its default and range, how it handles the edge cases, what it gives up by design, and where the build differs from its published source. It describes the protections that can stop a run whatever the law asks for, and how a reader can plug in a law of their own. Each law’s equations, as written here, were implemented again without Hertzforge and compared with the shipped law on small made-up structures: the largest difference is 5e-10 dB in the pseudo-inverse laws’ errors and 2e-7 relative in the minimum-drive law’s solve, except in the closed loop after 22 loops, where a change of one part in 10^15 moves single lines as far. The article reports no score. A companion article reports how each law scores on the Open Benchmark Set.

Summary

A random vibration test on one shaker has one control signal and one target spectrum. The control signal may be one accelerometer or a combination of several, an average for example; the controller raises or lowers the drive at each frequency until that signal reads its target. With several shakers on one article the controller has more to decide. Each shaker’s drive reaches every control accelerometer, each control accelerometer has a target spectrum of its own, and the target says not only how strong the vibration is at each of them but how the motion at one point is related to the motion at another. The rule that decides the drives is the control law.

Hertzforge ships six control laws for this kind of test. Five come from the published literature and are Kevin Cross’s implementations; the sixth, the band-average law, is Brandon Zwink’s. They differ in what they ask the shakers to do, how they use the measured response, and what they give up when the target cannot be met exactly.

This article describes each law as it is built, to the standard of a journal article: the problem it solves, its derivation, its algorithm in the order the code runs it, its settings, its edge cases, its design intent, and its published source with each place the build departs from it. Section 1 explains the control problem without equations beyond the one that defines it. Section 2 describes the closed loop four of the laws share, then each law. Section 3 describes the protections the controller applies to every law. Section 4 says how to plug in a new law. Section 5 reports how the descriptions were checked against the code, including a numerical check. Section 6 says what the article does not claim and the known limits of the build. Appendix A gives the derivations in full, and Appendix B carries one frequency line of a made-up structure through every law, with numbers a reader can reproduce from the article’s code.

Nothing here says how well a law does. That is the subject of the companion study (Cross 2026b), which cites this article for the laws.

1. The Control Problem in Plain Words

1.1 From One Shaker to Several

A random vibration target is a spectrum: how much vibration there is in each narrow slice of frequency, in units such as g²/Hz. The controller works on a grid of such slices, called lines, typically a fraction of a hertz to a few hertz apart. A range of lines grouped together, for example a one-third octave, is a band. A test on one shaker has one control signal, which may be one accelerometer or a combination of several (an average, for example), and one target spectrum for it.

With several shakers, each control accelerometer has a target spectrum of its own, and a spectrum per accelerometer is not the whole story. Two accelerometers can each read their target spectrum while moving together, in opposition, or independently. Two more numbers per pair and per line describe that relation. The coherence, from 0 to 1, says how much of the motion at one point is linearly tied to the motion at the other. The phase says whether, at that frequency, they move together (0 degrees), in opposition (180 degrees), or somewhere between. The spectrum of one accelerometer is its autospectrum; the quantity that carries the coherence and phase of a pair is their cross spectrum.

All of these, for every accelerometer and every pair, form one square table per line: the spectral density matrix. Its diagonal holds the autospectra and its off-diagonal entries the cross spectra. The same holds for the shaker drives: the drive is not one voltage spectrum per shaker but a matrix per line, whose off-diagonal entries say how correlated each pair of drives is. A spectral density matrix of real signals can never ask for negative power in any combination of its channels. A matrix with that property is called positive semi-definite, and a drive matrix without it cannot be played.

Four terms from the first article in this journal (Cross and Zwink 2026) are used throughout. The article is the test article, the hardware under test. A gauge is an accelerometer on it. The gauges the controller reads and brings to their targets are the control gauges. A held-out gauge is recorded but never shown to the controller; it is used only to judge the test. A system is the article on its suspension or fixture with a given set of shakers and control gauges, as the controller sees it (the first article of this series calls it the rig). Systems fall into three classes: as many shakers as control gauges (a square system), more control gauges than shakers, and fewer. A system is written shakers by control gauges: a 6 x 12 system has six shakers and twelve control gauges.

What the controller knows of the system is the measured frequency response: on each line, a matrix HH with one row per control gauge and one column per shaker, giving the acceleration at each gauge per volt of drive at each shaker (g/V), measured at the start of the run. If the drive matrix is GuuG_{uu} (V²/Hz), the response matrix at the control gauges (g²/Hz) is

Gyy=HGuuHH(1) G_{yy} = H \, G_{uu} \, H^{H} \qquad(1)

where HHH^{H} is the conjugate transpose of HH (Appendix A.1). A multi-shaker controller’s task is to choose GuuG_{uu} on every line so that GyyG_{yy} matches the target, GrefG_{ref}, as closely as the system allows. Everything that follows is a way of doing that. The convention G=E[XXH]G = E[X X^{H}] for a spectral density matrix is the one Smallwood uses for multi-shaker control (Smallwood 1999); as he notes, it is the complex conjugate of the cross spectrum as Bendat and Piersol define it. The published methods this article draws on are mapped in a survey in this journal (Cross 2026a), which this article cites for the prior work instead of repeating it.

1.2 A Run From Start to Finish

Figure 1 shows the order of a run at the software’s defaults. The settings named here are in config.rs of crates/hertzforge-mimo-random (Identification, Gates, Caps, Estimation, Protections), and the order is MimoRandomService::run_auto in service.rs.

  1. The noise floor. With the drives off, the controller records what the gauges read on their own (32 averaged frames by default).
  2. Identification. Every shaker is driven at once with random noise that is uncorrelated from shaker to shaker. The level starts at 0.01 V RMS and is stepped, at most eight times, until the control gauges sit within 1 dB of 20 dB under the target (RMS over the band, averaged over the gauges). From the drives and the responses, averaged over ten frames per shaker and at least 64, the controller computes the measured frequency response HH on every line. It also computes each control gauge’s multiple coherence, the share of the gauge’s response that the drives explain (1 when nothing but the drives moves it), and each line’s condition number, defined in Section 1.4.
  3. The gates. By default every control gauge’s multiple coherence, averaged over the band, must reach 0.5, and every line’s condition number must stay under 1,000. A run that fails a gate waits for the operator.
  4. The prediction. The control law computes its first drive from the identification, and the controller predicts what it will do before anything is played. A first drive over a shaker’s voltage ceiling waits for the operator.
  5. The soft start and the level steps. The loop starts 9 dB under the first level step and climbs to it over 5 s, then steps up, by default from -12 dB to full level in 3 dB steps held 3 s each.
  6. Full level. The loop runs at the target level until the test time is up or the operator stops it.

1.3 One Loop

From the soft start to the end, the controller repeats one loop (Figure 2), loop_tick in service.rs. It plays the drive in force, estimates the response matrix over the loop from 64 averaged frames (Hann window, half overlap), blends it into a running estimate with a memory of about four loops (a plain mean over the first two loops, then an exponential average that gives the newest loop a weight of 0.4), and refers it to full level: a response measured at -6 dB is scaled up by 6 dB before anything is judged or handed on. Every matrix the control law sees, and every drive it returns, is at full level. The controller owns the level, and a law cannot raise it.

Two kinds of loop are not handed to the law. The first loop after the loop engages is a settling loop: its estimate is discarded, and neither the abort lines nor the growing-error check of Section 3 judge it. A loop during which the level moved (the soft start’s climb) is not judged against the abort lines or for a growing error, and is not handed to the law; the drive in force is played at the moving level. The drive-voltage, kinematic and limit-channel aborts apply in every loop. A level step of the schedule is made between loops and does not count as a moving level.

On each call the law is given the target with its alarm and abort lines, the measured response matrix, the measured frequency response, each gauge’s multiple coherence, the estimate’s degrees of freedom, the line spacing, the band and the loop count. A law may also ask for the last drive played, the loop’s cross spectrum between response and drive, the identification’s response or drive, or the noise floor. It returns the next drive matrix and whatever diagnostics it declared. The interface is MimoControlLaw in crates/hertzforge-sdm (law module): seed for the first drive, iterate for every one after.

The measured frequency response the law is given is, by default, the one from identification: measured once before the run and kept. The test may instead have it measured again during the run: every loop the controller forms a new estimate from the played drive and the measured response and blends it with the identification’s by a weight the test sets (FrfMode::Update in config.rs).

1.4 What a System Can Reach

With as many shakers as control gauges and an exact frequency response, Equation 1 can be inverted line by line, and any target can in principle be met. With more control gauges than shakers it cannot: four shakers cannot set the spectra and cross spectra of twelve gauges independently. With fewer control gauges than shakers there are many drives that meet the target, and one has to be chosen.

The tool for all three cases is the pseudo-inverse H+H^{+} of the frequency response (V/g). The drive

Guu=H+GrefH+H(2) G_{uu} = H^{+} \, G_{ref} \, H^{+H} \qquad(2)

gives, with more gauges than shakers, the response closest to the target in the least-squares sense, which is the target projected onto what the system can produce, PGrefPHP G_{ref} P^{H} with P=HH+P = H H^{+}; with fewer gauges than shakers it gives, of all drives that meet the target exactly, the one of least total power (both derived in Appendix A.2). Smallwood uses the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse for both cases (Smallwood 1999). The distance between the target and what the system can reach is the declared deviation of the first article (Cross and Zwink 2026).

The pseudo-inverse is computed from the singular value decomposition of HH on each line, H=USVHH = U S V^{H}. In plain terms, the columns of VV are the patterns of shaker drive the system responds to, ordered from strongest to weakest, the diagonal of SS (the singular values s1≥s2≥…s_1 \ge s_2 \ge \ldots, g/V) says how strongly it responds to each, and the columns of UU are the responses those patterns produce at the gauges. The ratio s1/smins_1 / s_{min} is the line’s condition number. A large one means some drive pattern barely moves the gauges, so meeting a target in that direction takes a large drive, and a small error in HH becomes a large error in the drive. Regularization limits that: it drops or damps the weakest directions before inverting. With filter factors fif_i (V/g), the regularized pseudo-inverse is

H+=Vdiag⁡(f)UH,fi={1/sitruncation, si>αs10truncation, si≤αs1si/(si2+(αs1)2)Tikhonov damping(3) H^{+} = V \operatorname{diag}(f) \, U^{H}, \qquad f_i = \begin{cases} 1/s_i & \text{truncation, } s_i > \alpha s_1 \\ 0 & \text{truncation, } s_i \le \alpha s_1 \\ s_i / (s_i^2 + (\alpha s_1)^2) & \text{Tikhonov damping} \end{cases} \qquad(3)

with α\alpha the regularization amount, relative to the largest singular value (Schultz 2019). A truncation amount of 10−310^{-3} keeps the inverse’s condition number under 1,000. Schultz found input estimation errors growing large where the condition number exceeded about 1,000, and that too little or too much regularization both add error (Schultz 2019). In the code, Equation 3 is filter and inverse in crates/hertzforge-mimo-laws/src/support.rs. Every rule first drops singular values at or below max⁡(nc,nd)ϵs1\max(n_c, n_d)\,\epsilon\, s_1, with ncn_c and ndn_d the numbers of control gauges and shakers and ϵ\epsilon the machine precision, so an exactly singular line inverts nothing it cannot.

2.1 The Shared Closed Loop

What it does. A closed loop measures what the last drive produced and corrects the next one. Correcting the drive matrix directly can make it ask for negative power, which cannot be played. Smallwood’s method avoids that by never correcting the drive matrix itself (Smallwood 1999). It splits the target into a triangular factor, keeps a running copy of that factor, and corrects the copy from the error each loop; the drive is always formed from the copy in a way that cannot ask for negative power.

The problem. On each line, find a drive GuuG_{uu} that can be played and whose response matches the target:

Guu=AZZHAH,HAZZHAHHH=Gref(4) G_{uu} = A \, Z Z^{H} A^{H}, \qquad H A Z Z^{H} A^{H} H^{H} = G_{ref} \qquad(4)

where ZZ is a lower-triangular matrix with real, non-negative diagonal (g/√Hz) and AA maps the factor’s sources to the drives (V/g). At the start A=H+A = H^{+} and Z=RZ = R, the factor of the target, Gref=RRHG_{ref} = R R^{H}, so the first drive is Equation 2. A matrix of the form BBHB B^{H} never asks for negative power (Appendix A.3), so every drive the loop makes can be played.

The derivation. Each loop compares the measured response with the target, E=Gref−GyyE = G_{ref} - G_{yy} (g²/Hz), and looks for the change Δ\Delta in the factor that would remove that error. When the measured frequency response is exact and the system has as many independent directions as gauges, Gyy=ZZHG_{yy} = Z Z^{H}, and (Z+Δ)(Z+Δ)H=Gref(Z + \Delta)(Z + \Delta)^{H} = G_{ref} to first order in Δ\Delta is

ΔZH+ZΔH=E(5) \Delta Z^{H} + Z \Delta^{H} = E \qquad(5)

With Δ\Delta lower triangular and its diagonal real, Equation 5 is solved one column at a time, the diagonal first (Smallwood 1999) (Appendix A.4):

Δjj=Ejj−2∑m<jRe⁡(ΔjmZjm¯)2Zjj,Δkj=Ekj−∑m<j(ΔkmZjm¯+ZkmΔjm¯)−ZkjΔjjZjj,k>j(6) \Delta_{jj} = \frac{E_{jj} - 2\sum_{m<j} \operatorname{Re}(\Delta_{jm} \overline{Z_{jm}})}{2 Z_{jj}}, \qquad \Delta_{kj} = \frac{E_{kj} - \sum_{m<j}(\Delta_{km}\overline{Z_{jm}} + Z_{km}\overline{\Delta_{jm}}) - Z_{kj}\Delta_{jj}}{Z_{jj}}, \; k > j \qquad(6)

This is the first form of the correction, which changes the size and the phase of each term. Columns are solved in channel order, so autospectra come before the cross terms they feed, and the first control gauge has the highest priority. The update is Z←Z+eΔZ \leftarrow Z + e\,\Delta, with the convergence factor ee between 0 and 1.

A cross term of the factor is not allowed to grow when its gauge is already above its target. In that case the second form replaces the first: it changes only the term’s size, toward zero,

Δkj={ZkjEkk/(2Gref,kk)when −Ekk<Gref,kk/2−Zkj/2when −Ekk≥Gref,kk/2(7) \Delta_{kj} = \begin{cases} Z_{kj} \, E_{kk} / (2\, G_{ref,kk}) & \text{when } -E_{kk} < G_{ref,kk}/2 \\ -Z_{kj}/2 & \text{when } -E_{kk} \ge G_{ref,kk}/2 \end{cases} \qquad(7)

so a gauge more than half again over its target has the term moved halfway to zero before the convergence factor applies. This is the rule the build uses, not a derived step: if every term of the row were scaled by 1+δ1 + \delta, the gauge’s response would scale by about 1+2δ1 + 2\delta, and δ=Ekk/(2Gref,kk)\delta = E_{kk} / (2 G_{ref,kk}) would remove the relative excess to first order; the build applies that δ\delta to each cross term the second form governs, while the row’s diagonal takes the first form of its own column (Appendix A.4). Smallwood gives the second form as his Equation 13, with twice the row index times the column gauge’s target in the denominator and the column gauge’s error in the numerator. His appendix follows that form in the first column, and in the other columns uses the column index and the row gauge’s error and target; it moves the term by half of itself when the gauge is half again off its target, and uses the second form only for a cross term that is not zero (Smallwood 1999). Which of the two forms is used in the first column changes the result on the case of Figure 3 (Section 6). The build divides by 2Gref,kk2\, G_{ref,kk} in every column and applies the second form to a cross term at zero as well (Table 2).

The factorization allows a zero on its diagonal, which Smallwood calls a Cholesky factorization with zeros, citing Dodds and Robson (Smallwood 1999; Dodds and Robson 1975): a gauge whose target is fully coherent with gauges before it adds no source of its own. Smallwood sets the whole row and column of such a pivot to zero. The build counts a pivot at or below 10−1010^{-10} of its gauge’s target autospectrum as zero and sets only that column to zero for the whole run, so the gauge keeps the part of its target it shares with the gauges before it.

The coordinates. When the system carries fewer independent directions than there are control gauges (more gauges than shakers, or directions dropped by regularization or by the shape constraint), only part of the error can be corrected. The shipped loop then runs in the coordinates of what the system can produce. With qq the number of directions kept, T=UqHT = U_q^{H} reduces a gauge-space matrix to qq by qq, and A=Vqdiag⁡(fq)A = V_q \operatorname{diag}(f_q) maps the reduced sources to the drives. The loop factors and corrects TGrefTHT G_{ref} T^{H} against TGyyTHT G_{yy} T^{H}. That reduced loop is square, so on a system whose measured frequency response is exact it settles at the least-squares response PGrefPHP G_{ref} P^{H}, the response the open-loop pseudo-inverse gives (Appendix A.5). qq counts the singular values the inverse kept that are also above the rank tolerance (by default 10−610^{-6}) times the largest. In reduced coordinates, “gauge kk” in the rules below reads “source kk”: EkkE_{kk}, Gref,kkG_{ref,kk} and the hold act on the diagonal of the reduced matrices.

The algorithm as built. FactorLoop in factor.rs:

  1. FactorLoop::seed: form the inverse (support.rs, inverse); per line, choose the coordinates (LineMap::new), reduce and factor the target (factor_with_zeros), set Z=RZ = R, and return Guu=(AZ)(AZ)HG_{uu} = (AZ)(AZ)^{H} (FactorLoop::drive, a Gram matrix by gram_packed).
  2. FactorLoop::iterate, per line: if the measured frequency response changed since the last call, rebuild the coordinates and carry the factor over by factoring the law’s own last drive in the new coordinates, A+GuuA+HA^{+} G_{uu} A^{+H}, so the correction made so far is kept (FactorLoop::refresh).
  3. Reduce the measured response and form EE. A line with no target is skipped.
  4. Solve Equation 6 column by column, replacing a cross term by Equation 7 where its gauge is over target and the first form would grow it. The divisor is max⁡(Zjj,Zmin)\max(Z_{jj}, Z_{min}).
  5. Diagonal limit. No diagonal term of the factor may move by more than the per-loop limits of power, by default +3.5 dB and -6 dB (a factor element moves by half as many decibels). A column whose diagonal would move more has its first-form terms scaled down by the diagonal’s own ratio; second-form terms are applied as they are.
  6. Hold on a source that does not answer. When a source’s measured response, in the loop’s coordinates, is below 0.03 (-15 dB) of what its row of the factor commands, (TGyyTH)kk<0.03(ZZH)kk(T G_{yy} T^{H})_{kk} < 0.03\,(Z Z^{H})_{kk}, no element of that row may grow in that loop; each may still shrink.
  7. Update Z←Z+eΔZ \leftarrow Z + e\Delta.
  8. Bound on what a source commands. No row may command more than ten times (+10 dB) its source’s target, (ZZH)kk≤10(TGrefTH)kk(Z Z^{H})_{kk} \le 10\,(T G_{ref} T^{H})_{kk}, or more than it commanded the loop before where that was already higher; a row over the bound is scaled down to it.
  9. Floor. No diagonal term falls below Zmin=10−4max⁡k(TGrefTH)kkZ_{min} = 10^{-4}\sqrt{\max_k (T G_{ref} T^{H})_{kk}}; when one would, it is set to the floor, and the rest of its column is set to its values before this loop’s update, scaled by the ratio of the new diagonal to the old (clamped to 0 to 1); this loop’s correction to them is dropped.
  10. Drive limit. The step is halved, line by line, until no shaker’s drive autospectrum moves by more than the per-loop limits from the law’s last drive (a drive under 10−610^{-6} of the line’s largest is measured against that floor). After 30 halvings the line’s factor is held where it was.
  11. Return Guu=(AZ)(AZ)HG_{uu} = (AZ)(AZ)^{H}, with the diagnostics condition, kept, correction_db (the largest change of any drive autospectrum, dB) and clamped (diagonals held by step 5).

Settings. The convergence factor, the two per-loop limits and the rank tolerance are settings of the default law (Table 3); the other three laws that use this loop fix the limits at +3.5 and -6 dB and the rank tolerance at 10−610^{-6} (ShapeConstrainedLaw::new, BuzzMatchedLaw::seed, MinimumDriveLaw::seed).

Edge cases.

  • As many shakers as gauges, or fewer, at full rank: the loop runs in the gauges’ own coordinates with A=H+A = H^{+}, exactly as the source states it.
  • More gauges than shakers, or a line where the inverse dropped directions: the reduced coordinates above; the error outside what the system can produce is not corrected.
  • A rank-deficient or nearly singular line: the directions the inverse dropped are not sources; if no direction is kept, q=0q = 0 and the line’s drive is zero.
  • A tiny singular value that survives truncation: the hold (step 6) and the bound (step 8) stop the loop from raising the drive without limit where the structure does not answer.
  • A line with no target: skipped; its drive is zero.
  • The first loop: the seed is the pseudo-inverse drive; the first correction uses the first measured response the controller hands on, after the settling loop and the soft start’s climb.

2.4 Shape-Constrained (shape_svd)

What it does. On each line it drives only the mm patterns the system responds to most, the leading columns of VV, and gives up the rest. The weak directions are where a pseudo-inverse spends the most drive for the least response, so constraining the drive to the strong ones trades match between modes for drive. In the authors’ simulation of a beam with four shakers, two directions matched the target response closely, three needed much more force than two, and the method was nearly as accurate as the standard inverse with much lower forces (Schultz and Avitabile 2019).

The problem. With C=VmC = V_m, the first mm columns of VV on a line, the constrained system is Hc=HCH_c = H C, the constrained drive is Gc=Hc+GrefHc+HG_c = H_c^{+} G_{ref} H_c^{+H}, and the shaker drive is Guu=CGcCHG_{uu} = C G_c C^{H} (Schultz and Avitabile 2019).

The derivation. Because HC=UmSmH C = U_m S_m, the drive’s inverse is CHc+=VmSm−1UmHC H_c^{+} = V_m S_m^{-1} U_m^{H}, the pseudo-inverse truncated to exactly mm directions (Appendix A.6), which the authors point out themselves (Schultz and Avitabile 2019). The law is therefore the shared loop of Section 2.1 with an inverse that keeps mm directions, so no more than mm independent sources are ever driven, in the seed or in any loop.

The algorithm as built. The loop then runs in the reduced coordinates of the mm kept directions whenever mm is less than the number of gauges.

2.6 Minimum Drive (min_drive)

What it does. When the specification fixes only autospectra, the cross terms are free, and some choices need far less drive than others. This law chooses them. On each line it finds the drive that best trades two things against each other, the error in the autospectra and the total drive power, and makes the response the system would produce under that drive its target. The closed loop of Section 2.1 then holds that target.

The problem. On each line, with yky_k the specified autospectra (g²/Hz) and wkw_k their weights, the drive XX (V²/Hz) minimizes

∑kwk|yk−(HXHH)kk|+λtr⁡Xover positive semi-definite X(9) \sum_k w_k \left| \, y_k - (H X H^{H})_{kk} \, \right| \; + \; \lambda \operatorname{tr} X \quad \text{over positive semi-definite } X \qquad(9)

where tr⁡X\operatorname{tr} X is the total drive power and λ\lambda the price of a unit of drive (Coletti et al. 2026). The target is then HXHHH X H^{H}, the full matrix, cross terms included. By default the weights are wk=1/ykw_k = 1/y_k, so each gauge is judged by the fraction of its spectrum it misses, and the weighted error is dimensionless. As λ\lambda falls toward zero the solution becomes Smallwood’s minimum input trace: of all drives that meet the autospectra exactly, the one of least total power (Smallwood 2007; Smallwood 2013). Figure 6 (left) shows the principle on one line: with the autospectra held, the drive power depends on the cross term’s coherence and phase, and the least lies at full coherence and one phase (Appendix A.8).

The derivation. Equation 9 is convex. Writing each weighted absolute value as wk|rk|=max⁡|νk|≤wkνkrkw_k |r_k| = \max_{|\nu_k| \le w_k} \nu_k r_k and exchanging the minimum and the maximum gives its dual (Appendix A.8): maximize ∑kνkyk\sum_k \nu_k y_k over real ν\nu, subject to S(ν)=λI−HHdiag⁡(ν)HS(\nu) = \lambda I - H^{H} \operatorname{diag}(\nu) H positive semi-definite and |νk|≤wk|\nu_k| \le w_k. The dual has one real unknown per gauge where the primal has the square of the number of shakers. With a logarithmic barrier of weight μ\mu, the minimizing drive is X=μS−1X = \mu S^{-1}, and the barrier’s residuals vanish as μ\mu does.

The trade-off is given relative to the line, λ=λrelr0/d0\lambda = \lambda_{rel}\, r_0 / d_0, with r0=∑kwkykr_0 = \sum_k w_k y_k the weighted error of no drive at all and d0=∑kyk/‖hk‖2d_0 = \sum_k y_k / \lVert h_k \rVert^2 a drive power scale (hkh_k is gauge kk’s row of HH; yk/‖hk‖2y_k / \lVert h_k \rVert^2 is the least drive power that puts yky_k on gauge kk alone), so the same λrel\lambda_{rel} means the same on every system.

By default λrel\lambda_{rel} is chosen automatically. The grid λrel=10−6,10−5,…,106\lambda_{rel} = 10^{-6}, 10^{-5}, \ldots, 10^{6} is solved on a sample of the band’s lines, and for each grid point gg the drive power Dg=∑tr⁡XD_g = \sum \operatorname{tr} X and weighted error RgR_g are summed over the sample. Of the points left, the one with the smallest

(Dg+ε)(Rg+ε),ε=10−12(max⁡D+max⁡R)(10) (D_g + \varepsilon)(R_g + \varepsilon), \qquad \varepsilon = 10^{-12}(\max D + \max R) \qquad(10)

2.7 Band Average (band_average)

What it does. It matches averages over fractional-octave bands, one-sixth octave by default, instead of single lines. In each band it solves for one drive matrix, the same on every line of the band, whose band-averaged response matches the band-averaged target. The matrix is fitted whole, cross terms and all, so the drives come out correlated, uncorrelated or partly correlated as the fit needs. Averaging the frequency response over a band before inverting it keeps the noise in the identification from being amplified line by line. A threshold per band, chosen once at the first solve, sets how far each band’s inverse is regularized. After that a gain correction, band by band and gauge by gauge, moves each band’s target by the ratio of predicted to measured response. The law is Brandon Zwink’s.

The problem. For band bb (edges flf_l, fuf_u, width fu−flf_u - f_l in Hz) and control gauge pp, the response to a drive matrix PbP_b (V²/Hz) held flat across the band, averaged over the band, is

y‾b,p=1fu−fl∑k∈bwkhp(k)Pbhp(k)H(11) \bar{y}_{b,p} = \frac{1}{f_u - f_l} \sum_{k \in b} w_k \, h_p(k) \, P_b \, h_p(k)^{H} \qquad(11)

where hp(k)h_p(k) is gauge pp’s row of HH on line kk and wkw_k (Hz) is how much of line kk’s bin lies inside the band. Equation 11 is linear in the entries of PbP_b; its coefficients are the band sensitivity. Stacking the gauges (and, when cross terms are fitted, the band-averaged cross terms) gives a set of linear equations Abvec⁡(Pb)=tbA_b \operatorname{vec}(P_b) = t_b, with tbt_b the band-averaged target tb,p=∑k(wk/(fu−fl))Gref,pp(k)t_{b,p} = \sum_k (w_k / (f_u - f_l))\, G_{ref,pp}(k).

The derivation. The equations are solved through the singular value decomposition of AbA_b with the Tikhonov filter

vec⁡(Pb)=Vdiag⁡(ϕi)UHtb,ϕi=sisi2+(s1/κb)2(12) \operatorname{vec}(P_b) = V \operatorname{diag}(\phi_i)\, U^{H} t_b, \qquad \phi_i = \frac{s_i}{s_i^2 + (s_1/\kappa_b)^2} \qquad(12)

where κb\kappa_b is the band’s threshold: the larger it is, the closer the solve is to the plain pseudo-inverse. The solution is made Hermitian and then positive semi-definite by dropping negative eigenvalues. When only autospectra are fitted and that moves a predicted response by more than 0.1 dB, the band is fitted again over drives that ask for no negative power.

The thresholds are chosen at the first solve by a sweep. Every band is solved at 81 values of κ\kappa from 1 to 10⁵ (65 values from 10 to 10⁵ for the knee rule), and each band’s predicted mean decibel error over its gauges, e‾b(κ)\bar{e}_b(\kappa), and drive energy, ℰb(κ)=tr⁡Pb×∑kwk\mathcal{E}_b(\kappa) = \operatorname{tr} P_b \times \sum_k w_k, are recorded. The price is swept over 241 values from 10−610^{-6} to 10610^{6} times a reference price, and of the prices whose mean error over every gauge in every band is within 0.1 dB of the best, the one needing the least total energy is kept. The thresholds are then held for the run.

The gain correction compares, band by band and gauge by gauge, the measured response with the response the band sensitivity predicts from the drive actually played, and smooths the logarithm of their ratio,

ℓb,p←(1−r)ℓb,p+rln⁡ŷb,pyb,p,|ℓb,p|≤ln⁡100.6(13) \ell_{b,p} \leftarrow (1 - r)\,\ell_{b,p} + r \ln\frac{\hat{y}_{b,p}}{y_{b,p}}, \qquad |\ell_{b,p}| \le \ln 10^{0.6} \qquad(13)

where ŷb,p\hat{y}_{b,p} is the band average the band sensitivity predicts from the drive played and yb,py_{b,p} the measured band average, at a rate rr of 0.25 per call, capped at ±6 dB. The band’s target for that gauge is multiplied by eℓb,pe^{\ell_{b,p}}, and the bands are solved again when any multiplier has moved by more than 0.05 dB since the last solve. Comparing with the prediction rather than with the target means a change of drive the law made on purpose is never fought.

Figure 7 shows the first solve on one gauge and one shaker: the band averages meet the target, the lines inside a band do not, and in the one band where the drive is costliest the common price leaves the band 1.9 dB under its target.

  2. build_sensitivity and band_sensitivity: AbA_b of Equation 11 from the measured frequency response.

Edge cases. A band whose lines carry no target gets no drive, and lines inside the band where no gauge has a target get none either. With more gauges than shakers the band’s equations are solved by least squares; with fewer, the filtered minimum-norm solution. A rank-deficient band sensitivity is regularized by the threshold; under regularization none, singular values at or below the numerical floor are dropped. The first call returns the first solve; the gain correction starts at the second, with the drive the controller actually played (the law declares the input LastDrive). A gauge left out by the channel check stays in the solve with its gain multiplier reset to 1 until it comes back within the check.

What it gives up by design. It does not shape the response inside a band: inside a band with a resonance, a flat drive cannot follow the resonance (Figure 7), so line-by-line error stays where the band averages are met. The common price can leave a costly band short of its target. In exchange it does not chase line-by-line noise in the measured frequency response, and a small shift of a mode inside a band changes a band average less than it changes the lines. Decibel errors in fractional-octave bands are how multi-shaker tests are judged in parts of the published literature (Ross et al. 2017) and how the first article in this journal proposes a test be judged (Cross and Zwink 2026).

Two of the aborts judge the law rather than the article. The growing-error abort compares each gauge with what the system can reach (with more gauges than shakers, the least-squares response PGrefPHP G_{ref} P^{H} from the identification) rather than with the target, so a law is not stopped for failing to reach what cannot be reached. It reads each loop’s own estimate, because an average over loops hides a drive that swings from loop to loop. The change-cap abort catches a law that swings the drive faster than the controller lets it, even when the averaged response looks right.

Two of the messages say more than the checks test. The growing-error check counts loops that do not improve on the gauge’s best by 0.1 dB; its message says the error “grew”. The change-cap check counts 5 of the last 10 accepted law drives; its message says “loops in a row”. The checks are what is described here; the messages are quoted as the code has them.

The defaults are protective and conservative on purpose. They exist to protect the shakers, the amplifiers and the article; a default that stops a good run costs time, and one that lets a bad run go on can cost hardware. Widening one for a real test is the test engineer’s decision for that test’s hardware. In particular, the growing-error count runs through the level steps, so a law still settling when it reaches full level can be stopped there. This article recommends no new default. A simulator has no hardware to protect, and the companion study states how it set these checks and why (Cross 2026b).

4. Plugging In a Law

A control law is a separate file loaded at run time: a WebAssembly component written against the interface hertzforge:control-law@0.1.0, world random-mimo (wit/control-law/random-mimo.wit). The six built-in laws are written once against the same boundary; the controller runs them natively and builds the same source into law files, and a test holds each law file to its native law within 10⁻¹² relative (crates/hertzforge-mimo-laws/tests/parity.rs). The supported language is Rust, through Hertzforge’s law kit.

A law declares its parameters (each becomes an input box in the application, with its range and default), the system classes it supports, the optional inputs it needs and the diagnostics it reports. It implements two calls: seed, which returns the first drive from the identification, and iterate, which returns the next drive from each loop’s measurement. The worked example in the Hertzforge repository is the open-loop pseudo-inverse of Section 2.3 as a complete law crate to copy (crates/hertzforge-law-sdk/template/mimo-open-loop), built with cargo build --target wasm32-wasip2 --release.

The simulator runs any law. Before an imported law may drive a shaker, it must pass the controller’s check (crates/hertzforge-random-laws/src/mimo_check.rs): whole runs on six simulated articles (a modal article with four shakers and four control gauges, the same with measurement noise 30 dB under the smallest control gauge’s response at full level, a modal article with twelve control gauges and six shakers, the same run up a six-step level ladder, an ill-conditioned one and a rigid plate), under the controller’s own protections. Every case asks that each call was answered in time with a drive the checks accept and that no protection tripped (the controller’s own growing-error judgement is excused on the ill-conditioned article only). The four-shaker articles then ask every control gauge to come within 1 dB RMS of its target within 20 loops at full level; the twelve-gauge articles ask for a settled drive, each gauge’s error changing by under 0.5 dB from loop to loop over three loops and within 6 dB; the ill-conditioned article asks only that the law survive. The pass is recorded against the law file’s hash, so a changed file is checked again. A new law can also be run on the Open Benchmark Set (Cross 2026c), as the six are in the companion study (Cross 2026b).

6. Limits

What this article does not claim. It reports no score and no ranking, and says nothing about how well any law does on any test. Every figure and every number is from small made-up structures, chosen to show what a law does. Everything is code and simulation; nothing here has run on hardware. Where a law’s design intent is stated, it is intent, not a finding. The companion study (Cross 2026b) reports how each law scores on the Open Benchmark Set.

A gauge held high near a mode. Where the measured frequency response is off near a mode, the drive leaks into a gauge, the gauge reads high, and the cross term that could cancel the leak would have to grow. The second form forbids a cross term to grow while its gauge is high, so the loop cuts that gauge’s diagonal term to the floor and the gauge stays high by the leak. In the build this holds gauge 2 near 90 Hz in Figure 3 (Appendix B.8): +4.87 dB at 91 Hz at loop 30, with the independent target, a banded one, and a target with cross terms (coherence 0.64, phase 30 degrees) alike. Smallwood’s second form, as published, can hold a gauge high in the same way. A line-by-line port of his Appendix A (code/smallwood_port, its output in data/smallwood_port_output.txt), run on the same case at the same convergence factor of 0.5, holds 90.5 to 93 Hz at the build’s levels when the target has cross terms, and holds 91.5 and 92 Hz (+5.04 and +4.61 dB) with the independent target. With the independent target it releases the line at 91 Hz (+0.40 dB at loop 30) and brings gauge 2’s RMS error from 85 to 96 Hz to 1.54 dB, against the build’s 2.37 dB. Any one of four of the build’s departures, put alone into the port, holds the 91 Hz line again: the second form on a cross term at zero (+4.87 dB, and 2.37 dB from 85 to 96 Hz, the build’s figures), the second-form step without the index (+4.86 dB), the order of the two forms (+4.87 dB), or the limit on the diagonal (+4.77 dB); the build’s divisor alone does not (+0.40 dB). The release also depends on how the first-column line of his listing is read (as printed, +0.40 dB; read like the other columns, +4.86 dB) and on the convergence factor (at 1, his own example’s value, and at 0.25 the port holds the line, at +4.87 and +4.80 dB).

What the build gains from its departures on this case is as plain. Near 160 Hz the port does not hold gauge 2 steady: at 161 Hz it swings from -5.71 dB (loop 3) to +2.52 dB (loop 15) and ends at -4.23 dB, where the build holds +2.97 dB from loop 3 on. With the target that has cross terms and a convergence factor of 1, the port diverges near 413 Hz (+132.7 dB at loop 30, with 103 of its own warnings of a diagonal correction above 10⁶); the build does not. Near 160 Hz on the independent target the hold is the build’s own departure, the second form on a term at zero: gauge 2’s RMS error from 150 to 172 Hz is 1.12 dB with the independent target and 0.40 dB with the target that has cross terms. These are behaviors on one made-up case, reported as the port gave them; they are no general statement about Smallwood’s method or the build.
