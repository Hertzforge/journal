Abstract

A sine test with several shakers is controlled, as published, through a model of the test article measured at a low level before the test. This survey collects what is published: five forms of control law with their equations checked against the page, what the test standards ask of a sine test and of several exciters, what vendors and facilities say they do, the effect of sweep rate on a lightly damped resonance, how limits and notching are handled, what active noise control, helicopter vibration control and rotor balancing know about holding a tone with several actuators, and what nonlinear vibration testing does with feedback. The published laws are thin on evidence: one two-axis table test, two beam tests, one simulation and one run-to-run time-domain method, none on a structure whose resonances move with level. No standard read sets a phase tolerance or a sine clause for several exciters. Two pieces of arithmetic of our own, shown in full, tie the sources together: the number of its own time constants a mode is given while a sweep crosses its bandwidth, which shows that a correction alone cannot hold a lightly damped mode below a few hundred hertz at the rates tests are run; and the largest phase error of a low-level model at a resonance that has moved, which reaches 90 degrees, where a one-channel correction stops converging, after a move of one half-power bandwidth. Where the published work stops is stated gap by gap, worded to a search without subscription databases.

Summary

A sine test drives a test article with one tone, swept slowly through a frequency range or held at one frequency. With several shakers, the controller has to choose an amplitude and a phase for each shaker’s drive so that several accelerometers on the article each read what the test specification asks for. It does this from a model of the article measured with low-level noise before the test, and corrects the drives as the measured error comes in.

This article collects what is published about how that is done, so that a test engineer new to multi-shaker sine testing can see what exists and where it ends. It does not propose or rank control laws.

What we found:

  • Five published forms of control law, each given here with its equations as printed. Four correct the drive through a model measured once at a low level. One, a 1990 patent, changes its model as the test runs; we found no published test of its equations as printed. The evidence for all five is small: one two-axis table test, two beam tests, one simulation and one method that corrects between whole runs.
  • The standards set tolerances for each control channel, written for one shaker. None of those read has a sine clause for several exciters or a tolerance on the relative phase between control points.
  • Vendors and facilities describe their practice in words: a model refined during the test, a pseudo-inverse where the model is nearly singular, a limit met by scaling the whole reference down, phase control units that hold several shakers within 4 to 10 degrees. None publishes its law or the control error through a resonance. We found one published account of a swept sine controlled at points on a flexible article: a laboratory plate on four small shakers.
  • Sweep rate. A lightly damped mode needs time to build up. At the sweep rates the standards ask for, a mode with a Q of 50 below a few tens of hertz gets fewer than two of its own time constants while the tone is inside its bandwidth, and its peak comes late and low. We put the published sweep parameter into a form that can be checked with a stopwatch, and show from it that correcting after the error is measured cannot hold such a mode at those rates; the drive has to be moved ahead of the error.
  • Other fields that hold a tone with several actuators (noise cancellation, helicopter vibration control, rotor balancing) print the condition for a correction to work when its model is wrong: for one channel, a phase error under 90 degrees.
  • A structure that changes with level. Nonlinear vibration testing holds a tone through a moving resonance with feedback, but every such experiment we read uses one exciter. A low-level model’s phase error reaches 90 degrees once the resonance has moved one half-power bandwidth, which is 2 percent of the frequency for a Q of 50.

The published work stops short of a multi-shaker sine law with results on a structure whose resonances move with level, of any rule for carrying the model along a sweep, and of any rule for sharing a notch among several drives. Those statements are limited to our search, which had no subscription databases.

1. What a Sine Test With Several Shakers Is

A sine test drives a structure with one tone at a time. The test article (or simply the structure) is the thing being tested, mounted on a fixture or a slip table. Shakers apply the motion. Control accelerometers on the fixture or on the article measure the response, and the controller compares that response, at the tone, with the reference: the amplitude, and sometimes the phase, that the test specification asks for at each control point. From the difference it sets the drive, the voltage sent to each shaker’s amplifier.

The tone is moved in one of three ways. In a swept sine the frequency moves continuously, usually exponentially with time, so that the rate is stated in octaves per minute. In a dwell the frequency is held, for a time or a count of cycles, often at a resonance. In a stepped sine the frequency moves in steps and the controller lets the response settle at each step. Before and after the main run most tests make a low-level sweep, a resonance search, to find the article’s resonances and to see whether they moved.

Several shakers are used when one is not enough: an article too large or too heavy for one shaker, a long article whose ends need different motion, a table that must move in more than one axis, or a test that must hold rotation and cross-axis motion down. On a stiff fixture the shakers can often be run as one, in phase, with a small correction for their differences. On a flexible article, or near the fixture’s own resonances, they cannot: each shaker’s drive must differ in amplitude and phase from the others, and the right difference changes with frequency.

The control problem in one line. Call the number of shakers (drives) mm and the number of control channels nn. At one frequency, on a structure that behaves linearly and has settled to the tone, the complex amplitudes of the controls CC (a list of nn numbers, each an amplitude and a phase) follow from the complex drive amplitudes DD (a list of mm) through the structure’s frequency response matrix HH, an nn by mm table of how much each control moves per volt of each drive:

C=HD.C = H D.

Every source that states a multi-shaker sine law poses the problem this way (Musella and others 2019; Qiu and others 2019; Zhang, Chen and He 2015). The controller does not know HH; it holds a model Ĥ\hat{H}, usually measured before the test by driving all shakers at once with low-level random noise that is uncorrelated from shaker to shaker. The NASA Mechanical Vibration Facility does this before a sine sweep (Winkel and others 2017). A second published way measures HH with sine itself: the sweep is run pp times with different, independent patterns of drive phase, and HH is solved from the responses, H=UD−1H = U D^{-1}, with UU the responses and DD the drive patterns side by side (Zhang, Chen and He 2015, Eqs. 12 to 14). It costs pp runs at each level.

The feedforward drive is the model’s inverse applied to the reference, D0=ZRD_0 = Z R, where ZZ is the inverse of Ĥ\hat{H}, or its pseudo-inverse when there are more controls than shakers. The patent literature calls ZZ the impedance matrix. Feedback then corrects DD from the error E=R−CE = R - C, measured by a tracking filter: a narrow filter that follows the tone and gives each channel’s amplitude and phase at the drive frequency, for example by correlating the signal with a sine and a cosine at that frequency (Zhang, Chen and He 2015, Eqs. 7 to 9) or by a least-squares fit over a few periods (Musella and others 2018).

The reference may give amplitude and phase at every control, with the phase stated relative to the first control channel (Musella and others 2019), or amplitude only, leaving the relative phases free (Zhang, Chen and He 2015). What goes wrong is also common to the sources: the model is measured at a low level and the structure at test level is not the same, and a sweep never lets a lightly damped resonance settle to the steady state that C=HDC = H D describes. Sections 5 and 8 return to both.

Two things stand out. First, every tolerance is written per channel or for a composite of the channels; none of the documents read sets a tolerance on the relative phase between control points, and none has a sine clause for several exciters. Two say only that the fixing points move “substantially in phase”, which is a qualitative statement written for one shaker. Second, the handbook’s advice on control strategy (average or maximum of the pilots, how many channels to close the loop on) is the single-axis practice. A multi-shaker sine test is therefore specified today by carrying the single-shaker tolerances over to each control, and by whatever the test’s own procedure adds.

We did not read NASA GSFC-STD-7000, SMC-S-016, the NASA handbooks on vibration testing, IEST-RP-DTE022 “Multi-shaker Test and Control”, the 2015 edition of ISO 7626-2, or the parts of IEC 60068-2-6 beyond its free preview. The levels of ECSS-E-ST-10-03C are factors from ECSS-E-ST-32-10 times a limit load spectrum, and we did not read those factors.

3. The Published Control Laws

We found five published forms of a multi-shaker sine law with their equations. Each is given below as its source prints it, then in one notation: DkD_k is the drive vector at control update kk, CkC_k the controls the tracking filters measured, Ek=R−CkE_k = R - C_k the error, ZZ the inverse of the model, and gg a scalar gain between 0 and 1. Every equation below was checked against the page of its source.

3.1 Correction Through a Fixed Inverse

The simplest law measures the model once, before the test, and corrects the drive through its inverse at every update:

Dk+1=Dk+gZ(R−Ck).D_{k+1} = D_k + g\, Z\, (R - C_k).

Qiu and others (2019, Eq. 10) print it as Xnew=Xold+βH−1EX_{new} = X_{old} + \beta H^{-1} E with 0≤β≤10 \le \beta \le 1, with the model H=GXX−1GYXH = G_{XX}^{-1} G_{YX} estimated from random noise (their Eq. 1). Zhang, Wang and Zeng (2017, Eqs. 15 to 18) start from D0=H−1RD_0 = H^{-1} R, call G=H−1G = H^{-1} “the impedance matrix”, and correct with Dk=Dk−1+cGEk−1D_k = D_{k-1} + c\, G\, E_{k-1}. Of the factor cc they write (our translation) that “in the sine amplitude correction process an excessive error often leads to over-correction of the excitation, producing a shock effect”, so a compression factor cc between 0 and 1 is introduced to slow the correction; as cc approaches 1 “the convergence accuracy is high but the convergence speed is slower”, and as it approaches 0 the opposite; 0.4 to 0.6 is usual. The 1990 patent discussed next describes the same law as the prior art it improves on, Dn+1=Dn+gZm(R−Cn)D_{n+1} = D_n + g Z_m (R - C_n), with ZmZ_m “measured and determined prior to beginning the first test cycle”, and notes that “negative values of g have to be used when phase errors greater than 90 degrees are present in measured values of the system response matrix” (Underwood, US 5,299,459, cols. 5 and 6).

That note has a short explanation, which is ours. With a fixed ZZ the error obeys Ek+1=(I−gHZ)EkE_{k+1} = (I - g H Z) E_k. It shrinks from update to update only if every eigenvalue λ\lambda of HZH Z satisfies |1−gλ|<1|1 - g\lambda| < 1. With one channel, HZ=ρejθH Z = \rho e^{j\theta}, and the condition is 0<gρ<2cos⁡θ0 < g \rho < 2 \cos\theta: there is no positive gain that works once the model’s phase is more than 90 degrees from the structure’s. Section 8 shows how easily that happens at a resonance that moves.

Evidence. Qiu and others (2019) ran a two-axis sine test on a three-shaker 60 kN table, 5 to 200 Hz at 2 octaves a minute, X at 0 degrees and Y at 90 degrees: “most part of the magnitude control curve are well within 10 % alarm tolerances and the phase control curve are well within ±10° alarm tolerances except for several level control points nearly 80 Hz as a result of the platform modes”. Zhang, Wang and Zeng (2017) controlled sine plus random on an aluminium cantilever beam.

3.2 An Adaptive Model and a Complex Gain: US 5,299,459

Underwood’s patent, “Adaptive control method for multiexciter sine tests” (filed 1990, granted 1994, listed as expired), keeps the drive update above but changes the model as the test runs. The initial model is found “by stimulating the system under test 12 sequentially with individual exciters 30 and measuring the response of the system 12 at each sensor 32” (col. 5). Each control cycle has two steps. In the learning step a trial drive is sent,

D̂n=Dn+cnZn(R−Cn),\hat{D}_n = D_n + c_n Z_n (R - C_n),

and the structure answers with Ĉn\hat{C}_n. From that answer a complex gain is computed (EQU2, col. 8),

an=cn⟨R−Cn,Ĉn−Cn⟩|Ĉn−Cn|2,a_n = c_n \frac{\langle R - C_n,\; \hat{C}_n - C_n \rangle}{| \hat{C}_n - C_n |^2},

and the control step applies Sn=anZn(R−Cn)=Dn+1−DnS_n = a_n Z_n (R - C_n) = D_{n+1} - D_n (col. 7). The model is then updated so that it maps the observed change in the controls onto the step that caused it (the secant condition Zn+1(Cn+1−Cn)=SnZ_{n+1}(C_{n+1} - C_n) = S_n, col. 7), by EQU1 (col. 6):

Zn+1=(IN+(Sn−Zn(Cn+1−Cn))Sn*⟨Zn(Cn+1−Cn),Sn⟩)Zn.Z_{n+1} = \left( I_N + \frac{\left(S_n - Z_n (C_{n+1} - C_n)\right) S_n^{*}}{\langle Z_n (C_{n+1} - C_n),\; S_n \rangle} \right) Z_n .

(In the patent’s printed learning-loop equation the left side carries no circumflex; the “where” list defines D̂n\hat{D}_n. All quantities are per frequency.) The trial gain cnc_n starts near 0.1 “in order to minimize control errors associated with using a possibly erroneous impedance estimate” and is “allowed to approach 1.0” as confidence grows, measured by ana_n, which the patent calls “the best indicator of the instant reliability” of the model. The patent states that the update converges to the true impedance in at most NN steps for a linear, time-invariant structure, once marked “(hypothetically)” and once without the qualifier (“The process is essentially stable and, converges in at most N iterations for linear and time invariant systems 12”); that for a nonlinear or time-varying structure “the controller 10 will not converge exactly in a finite number of steps”; and that the method “does potentially increase control error during initial iterations due to potential errors in initial values assigned to the system response matrix 44. Careful selection of values for c_n minimizes this problem” (cols. 7 and 8). The complex gain is presented as a refinement of using a negative gain where the phase error passes 90 degrees.

Evidence. The patent reports no test. We found no published test or simulation of EQU1 and EQU2 as printed. Musella and others (2018, Fig. 8) show a quasi-Newton controller that, as they describe it, combines the patent’s idea with an optimization method for complex variables, started from an identity matrix; it is their own implementation, not the printed equations. Two articles by Underwood and Keller (2001) and by Underwood, Keller and Ayres (2017) say the production law of their controller refines the model during the closed loop by this method (Section 4).

3.3 Steepest Descent

Musella and others (2019) treat the drive as the unknown of a least-squares problem, cost J=eHeJ = e^H e, and step down its gradient, un+1=un+αHHenu_{n+1} = u_n + \alpha H^H e_n (their Eqs. 9 to 11, with uu the drive and ee the error). The step’s poles are 1−ασi21 - \alpha \sigma_i^2, with σi\sigma_i the singular values of HH (Eqs. 17 to 20), and the law they recommend is (Eq. 22)

un+1=un+K1σ̂max2ĤHen,0<K<1,u_{n+1} = u_n + K \frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{max}^2} \hat{H}^H e_n, \qquad 0 < K < 1,

which in our notation is Dk+1=Dk+(g/σ̂max2)ĤH(R−Ck)D_{k+1} = D_k + (g / \hat{\sigma}_{max}^2)\, \hat{H}^H (R - C_k). The printed bound is 0≤α≤1/λmax0 \le \alpha \le 1/\lambda_{max} with optimum 1/σmax21/\sigma_{max}^2 (Eq. 21), while the caption of their Fig. 5 states 2/σmax22/\sigma_{max}^2; their book chapter on the same work (Musella and others 2020, read in part through page summaries) states a bound for each principal coordinate of HH separately. The authors are plain about the assumption: their analysis is “a condition at the limit of stability, obtained considering that all the transients have died out”, which “can be a strong assumption for a swept sine test”, and “no link has been theoretically made between the convergence and the sweep rate” (Section 5.1).

Evidence. Simulation of two uncoupled virtual electrodynamic shakers, 10 to 2500 Hz, a linear sweep of 2 Hz a second, with a 1 percent mass change between identification and test and step disturbances, from which the loop recovered in about half a second to a second.

3.4 An Amplitude-Ratio Law

Zhang, Chen and He (2015) control the amplitudes of several points and leave their phases free. The drive starts from the measured model, D=H−1rD = H^{-1} r; the model itself is measured by the phase-varied sine runs of Section 1, H=UD−1H = U D^{-1}. With DkD_k a drive amplitude, AkA_k the response and ArA_r the reference, they define Eq=Ar/AkE_q = A_r / A_k and print (Eq. 15)

Dk+1=DkK+1/EqK+Eqif Eq≤1,Dk+1=DkK+1K+Eqif Eq>1,D_{k+1} = D_k \frac{K + 1/E_q}{K + E_q} \;\; \text{if } E_q \le 1, \qquad D_{k+1} = D_k \frac{K + 1}{K + E_q} \;\; \text{if } E_q > 1,

with “K … the correction factor, whose size determines the accuracy and speed of convergence” (our translation); their Fig. 4 shows KK of 0.5, 1, 2 and 6, faster for smaller KK. A note of our own on the equation as printed: with EqE_q defined as the reference over the response, the second branch lowers the drive when the response is below the reference. The paper’s Fig. 4 plots the ratio of response to reference, so the printed definition may be the other way up; we give the equation as the paper prints it. The paper sets its reference as “amplitude and phase”, and Eq. 15 then corrects amplitude only; the authors leave phase control to further research.

Evidence. Two small electrodynamic shakers under a stainless steel beam 1115 by 50 by 14 mm, drives and controls at 143 and 376 mm, alarm at +/-3 dB and abort at +/-6 dB; both points were held within tolerance with some oscillation at the low and high ends of the band.

3.5 A Correction Between Whole Runs Through a Time-Domain Inverse

Zheng, Chen and Luo (2020) work in time. They estimate a multistep prediction model of the structure, invert it with a truncated singular value decomposition (“the direct inverse of the T matrix might induce numerical instability”), and run the reference time histories through the inverse to make the drives. The correction is made offline, between complete runs: after each run, at each frequency, the reference fed to the inverse is scaled by the ratio of the wanted to the measured response spectrum, raised to a power ε\varepsilon between 0 and 1 (their Eq. 29, Rn+1=Rn(R0/Sy)εR_{n+1} = R_n \, (R_0 / S_y)^{\varepsilon}). They advise controlling at a lower level first, “then restore the original level test”. On a three-axis shaker with two axes driven and controlled, at -6 dB, “After 4 iteration loops, the response spectra are controlled within acceptable tolerances”; at full level the spectra “become slightly worse, but they are still within the alert limits”. This is not a correction within the sweep. The authors describe the 1990 patent as “a patented frequency-domain control method with the tracking filters”, and know of no multi-shaker swept sine controlled by a time-domain method before theirs.

3.6 Sine on Random With Several Shakers

Zhang, Wang and Zeng (2017) control sine plus random on several shakers: the sine is separated by a correlation integral at its known frequency, the random part is controlled through its spectra, and the sine through the compressed impedance law of Section 3.1. Ma, Chen and Zheng (2022; 2024) treat multi-axis swept sine on random and sine on random; we read only their abstracts and say nothing of their update equations. MIL-STD-810H (2019) stated that no commercial multi-input multi-output solution existed for sine on random other than time waveform replication (Table 2).

4. What Vendors and Facilities Say They Do

Vendor articles. The most detailed public text from the commercial side is by Marcos Underwood and Tony Keller, in two articles in Sound and Vibration (2001, read in full; 2017 with Russ Ayres, read from section G to the end, since the copy we found lacks its opening pages). They state that in multi-shaker sine the frequency response matrix “is obtained initially and refined during the closed loop process by the same adaptive control method”, the 1990 patent’s, and they define real-time adaptive control as control “which updates the System Impedance Matrix (SIM) every loop to assure true control of non-linear responses rather than only updating the SIM at the beginning of the test” (2001). Where the matrix is close to singular, “If the ratio of the smallest to the largest singular value falls below a certain singularity threshold, the matrix is declared singular and a pseudo-inverse calculation is performed”; the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse “mitigates the tendency for the actuators to ‘fight’ and also limits the magnitude of the drives used for control”. Square control has “one phase/amplitude control transducer per shaker”, with any further channels used for “limit control of amplitudes, without affecting the phase parameters”. No sine equation is printed in either article.

The 2017 review adds three points. Multi-shaker swept sine for satellites “has so far been primarily limited to vertical testing using four shakers”, where “the requirement is to keep the four Z responses in phase and at the same level”, and analog phase control “may not be able to operate correctly past the first flexible body resonance”. In an eight-actuator sweep of a dummy mass in the Y axis, “the Z-axis drives are now out of phase in pairs”, which the authors credit with cancelling the roll from the overturning moment. And of sine on random: “Those who have conducted single-shaker tests using swept-sine-on-random or mixed-mode profiles know the difficulties that can be encountered in trying to accurately control moving and sometimes overlapping sine tones and harmonics. Now expand this concept to multiple shakers and even multiple axes and throw in the need for realizable and achievable phase control with cross-coupling compensation.” Their papers on rectangular control, on response limiting during multi-shaker testing, and on an eight-actuator satellite qualification system exist; we did not obtain them.

A vendor page. Data Physics’ page on multi-shaker control, fetched on 2026-11-15, describes “Realtime active notching, optimized to maintain stability and minimize overshoot”; “Preemptive notching begins notching when a notch channel’s slope indicates it will soon exceed its profile”, “preventing all control/limit channels from even reaching +3dB of the reference/notch profile. The only phase excursions happen when notching dips the control amplitude into the noise”; and a two-shaker case where “Two control points were controlled to be kept in-phase near the armature’s attachment points. Notching was applied to off-axis accelerometers.” No method is given. Other vendor text this project had read earlier could not be found again when checked, and we do not quote it.

An open controller. The open-source multi-axis vibration controller of Sandia National Laboratories is described in a 2021 paper (Rohe, Schultz and Hunter) that covers random, transient and time-history environments and no sine control, and in 2022 course slides (Rohe 2022) that list “Plans to expand to MIMO Sine”. We did not read its source code or documentation.

Facilities. The NASA Mechanical Vibration Facility “can support up to eight drive signals (i.e., independent references), up to twelve control accelerometers (i.e., control inputs), up to a total of 32 response limiting channels, and another 32 monitoring channels”, and for X-axis shakes “all twenty actuators are active and driven by eight source signals” (Staab and others 2016). Before a sine sweep “a low level random pretest characterization test run is performed with the 8 drive signals being uncorrelated so the MVF vibration controller can identify the dynamics of the integrated system” (Winkel and others 2017). Qiu and others (2022) survey several facilities: ESTEC’s four-shaker QUAD “sends one drive signal at the input of the amplifiers which is common to the four systems”, with an analog phase control unit “within +/-4 deg”; a dual-shaker system of two 200 kN shakers at BISEE in China, “within +/-10 deg”; BISEE’s four-shaker 1400 kN system, “within +/-5 deg”, whose phase control unit will “shut down and decrease the drive signals quickly to zero if the phase difference among the shakers beyond the abort value”. Of the Mechanical Vibration Facility they write that its controller “must cancel any cross axis or rotational vibration induced by load imbalance or structural resonance by setting the other unwanted reference spectrum at a very low level”, and that it is “capable of both acceleration and force limiting”. The design paper for ESTEC’s hydraulic six-axis shaker (Brinkman and Kretz 1992) describes sine and sine sweep among its test modes, an accuracy of “± 2,5 % of the input signal”, and on-line detection of the test item’s “resonance frequencies … to reduce the input level automatically (notching)”; it is a design-stage text. None of these papers states the control law used or reports the control error through a resonance.

Talks. Raman Sridharan of Data Physics taught a two-hour course, “Introduction to Multi-Shaker Testing”, at the 94th Shock and Vibration Symposium (2024), and gave a talk at the 34th Aerospace Testing Seminar (2025) on coupled multi-actuator sine testing at the Mechanical Vibration Facility. We found only the event listings; their content is not published.

Swept sine controlled on a flexible article. The only published account we found is in Underwood and Keller (2001): “We also ran some MIMO-sine tests with four shakers. In this case the aluminum plate was not as rigidly attached to the shakers.” The plate is “approximately 2 ft square”, on “four mini electrodynamic shakers”, swept from 5 to 80 Hz, and the results are given as plots of control and drive amplitude and phase, with no tolerance stated and nothing said about level. It is a laboratory demonstration, not a qualification test. In my experience, multi-shaker swept sine controlled at points on a flexible test article has not been tried much. What I have run is sine on random with several shakers, with the sweep controlled at fairly rigid fixture points.

5. Sweep Rate, Resonance and the Control Loop

A swept tone passes through a resonance in a few seconds, and a lightly damped resonance needs time to build up. This section puts the published results on that into one set of numbers a test engineer can check with a stopwatch, and then asks what that means for a control loop. The steps that are published are cited; the others are our own arithmetic, and are marked so.

Seconds inside the bandwidth (ours). A logarithmic sweep at ss octaves a minute changes frequency at ḟ=fsln⁡2/60\dot{f} = f s \ln 2 / 60 hertz a second. A mode at frf_r with quality factor Q=1/(2ζ)Q = 1/(2\zeta), where ζ\zeta is its damping ratio, has a half-power bandwidth fr/Qf_r / Q: the band over which its response is within 3 dB of the peak. The tone spends

Tb=fr/Qḟ=60Qsln⁡2≈86.6Qs secondsT_b = \frac{f_r / Q}{\dot{f}} = \frac{60}{Q\, s \ln 2} \approx \frac{86.6}{Q\, s} \text{ seconds}

inside that band, the same at every frequency: 1.7 s for a QQ of 50 at one octave a minute. The number of cycles inside it, frTbf_r T_b, grows with frequency: 17 at 10 Hz, 173 at 100 Hz. ECSS-E-HB- 10-03A (A.7.3.3) prints the same arithmetic: “1 octave per minute means 8,66 cycles per 0,1 Hz”.

The mode’s own time constant (ours). A mode’s free response decays as e−ζωrte^{-\zeta \omega_r t}, so its amplitude time constant is τm=1/(ζωr)=Q/(πfr)\tau_m = 1/(\zeta \omega_r) = Q/(\pi f_r): 1.6 s at 10 Hz and 0.40 s at 40 Hz for a QQ of 50. It is roughly the time the mode takes to build up to a new steady amplitude.

How many time constants the mode gets (ours, tied to a published parameter). The ratio

N=Tbτm=60πfrQ2sln⁡2≈272frQ2sN = \frac{T_b}{\tau_m} = \frac{60 \pi f_r}{Q^2 s \ln 2} \approx \frac{272 f_r}{Q^2 s}

is the number of its own time constants the mode is given while the tone is inside its bandwidth. It is the published sweep parameter in other words. Davis, Durham and Brown (2010, Eq. 10) plot the ratio of a single mode’s peak swept response to its steady response against η=Q2K/(60fn2)\eta = Q^2 K / (60 f_n^2), with KK the sweep rate in hertz a minute, and find the curve “insensitive to changes in oscillator natural frequency and damping, provided the damping ratio is low (< 0.03)”; they give the curve as consistent with the earlier work of Lollock (2002), which we did not read. With ḟ=K/60\dot{f} = K/60, η=Q2ḟ/fr2\eta = Q^2 \dot{f} / f_r^2 and N=π/ηN = \pi / \eta. Applying Davis’s parameter, written for a linear sweep, at the local rate of a logarithmic sweep is our step; Cronin (1965, read as its abstract only) reports that resonant behaviour depends mainly on the rate of change of frequency at the resonance. The ECSS loads handbook uses ηE=(Rln⁡2/60)/(fkζk2)\eta_E = (R \ln 2 / 60)/(f_k \zeta_k^2) with RR in octaves a minute (ECSS-E-HB-32-26A, 5.2.3.3), which is four times Davis’s η\eta (our derivation; same physics, a different constant).

What the structure does when NN is small (published). ECSS-E-HB-32-26A (5.2.3.3): “A frequency sweep decreases the amplitude of the peak, shifts the position of the peak along the direction of the sweep, broadens the peak width and distorts the shape of the peak”, effects “proportional to the sweep rate, R, and inversely proportional to the natural frequency f k and (approximately) to the square of the damping”. For a mode at 10 Hz with a QQ of 50 swept at one octave a minute (NN = 1.1 in Table 4), the handbook reports that the frequency and damping identified from the swept response by the half-power method have “2 % error in frequency but over 70 % error in damping”. ISO 7626-2:1990 (clause 9.2.3, read through IS 14736 (Part 2):1999, which states it is identical) requires a measurement sweep slow enough that, within 10 percent of a resonance, the measured response “is within 5 % of the true value”: no faster than 54fn2/Q254 f_n^2 / Q^2 hertz a minute for a linear sweep and 77.6fn/Q277.6 f_n / Q^2 octaves a minute for a logarithmic one. In the terms above that is η\eta under 0.9, or NN over 3.5 (ours). For a 40 Hz mode with a QQ of 50 it allows 1.2 octaves a minute.

The rates tests are run at (published, and my experience). IEC 60068-2-6 asks for one octave a minute. ECSS-E-ST-10-03C Rev.1 asks for 2 octaves a minute at qualification and 4 at acceptance and protoflight. ECSS-E-HB-10-03A (A.7.3.3) says “A high sweep rate (e.g. up to 4 octaves/min) is selected to reduce the number of cycles when the flight hardware is tested”, and also that “A low sweep rate usually makes the control easier and leads to higher amplification on the modes. To solve control issues, it is possible to modify the sweep rate locally around a mode”. In my experience 4 octaves a minute is not unheard of, but it is at the upper end for aerospace testing. Table 4 shows what those rates mean for a lightly damped mode: at 2 octaves a minute NN for a mode with a QQ of 50 is under 2 below 37 Hz, so a test article whose first modes are in the tens of hertz is swept through them before they settle.

What a correction-only loop can and cannot do (ours). A law of Section 3.1 or 3.3 holds the control by correcting the error after it is measured. Inside the bandwidth, the drive that would hold the control turns through 90 degrees and changes by 3 dB on each side of its minimum, a relative change of about 2 over the time TbT_b, faster than anywhere else in the sweep. A correction that removes the error with a time constant τ\tau (for the laws above, τ=Δt/g\tau = \Delta t / g, with Δt\Delta t the time between updates) follows a drive that changes at that rate with a standing error of about 2τ/Tb2\tau / T_b of the reference, which is 2(τ/τm)/N2 (\tau / \tau_m) / N. The loop cannot be made fast enough to shrink that freely: a correction faster than the mode can respond reads the mode’s own build-up as error, which is the assumption Musella and others name in Section 3.3. With the loop held to between one and two of the mode’s time constants, a correction alone leaves a standing error of roughly 2/N2/N to 4/N4/N of the reference.

How fast the loop may safely run is our round figure, not a published one, so read this as an order of magnitude. At 40 Hz, a QQ of 50 and one octave a minute (NN = 4.3) it is half to nine tenths of the reference. For an error near a tenth of the reference (about 1 dB), NN must be 20 to 40, which for a QQ of 50 at one octave a minute means above about 180 to 370 Hz, and twice that at 2 octaves a minute. A correction alone cannot hold a lightly damped mode below a few hundred hertz at the rates tests are run, and no setting of its gain changes that.

Carrying the drive along the sweep. In my experience it is common practice with one shaker to move the drive ahead of the error: as the tone moves, the controller changes the drive by what the measured frequency response predicts, and the correction takes care of the rest. The usual control setting is the share of the drive’s change that is taken from the measured response. We found no published multi-shaker sine law that says how its model, its gain or its correction is carried from one frequency to the next during a continuous sweep, and Musella and others state that no link between convergence and sweep rate had been made. Where NN is small a carried drive is not enough either, because the structure is not in the steady state that any model of C=HDC = H D describes: in the handbook’s case above (NN = 1.1) the frequency of the peak is 2 percent off, which for a QQ of 50 is one whole bandwidth. Slowing the sweep around the mode, as the ECSS handbook suggests, is the remedy that is published.

6. Limits and Notching With Several Shakers

A limit channel is an accelerometer or force gauge, not a control, with a bound the test must not exceed; notching is “reduction of the input level or spectrum to limit structural responses at resonant frequencies according to qualification or acceptance loads” (ECSS-E-ST-10-03C Rev.1, 3.2.15). The standard asks that “Notching criteria and implementation shall be approved by the customer” and that “Automatic protection measures shall be implemented during the test to prohibit excessive resonance build-up leading to hardware damage”, “by means of abort and notch accelerometers control” (6.5.2.8). IEC 60068-2-6 (4.2.2) states the multi-reference rule for limiting: “All control signals shall be beneath their appropriate reference”. The ECSS testing handbook warns that “Increasing the number of channels in the control loop can slow down and degrade the control” and recommends “not to change the parameters for high vibration levels if they have not been tested at lower level” (A.7.3.2). All of this is written for one shaker.

With one shaker there is one drive to lower. With several, a limit can be met in more than one way: lower every drive together, lower only the drives that excite the limit channel, or change the reference so that the controls ask for less of the motion that loads the limit point. Each changes the test differently. What is published:

  • Underwood, Keller and Ayres (2017): “to preserve the prespecified coherence and phase relationships between control-channels, it was sufficient to reduce the magnitude of either the reference vector or the reference spectral density … at those frequencies where a limit level is being exceeded. Additionally, this limit control method can be used for both MIMO random and MIMO sine applications”; the method “reduces the magnitude of the reference the controller is using in proportion to the particular limit exceedance”. That is one factor applied to the whole reference, chosen because it keeps the relative phases. Their results are shown for random.
  • Underwood and Keller (2001): channels beyond one per shaker used for “limit control of amplitudes, without affecting the phase parameters”.
  • Data Physics (page fetched 2026-11-15): real-time and preemptive notching, the latter starting “when a notch channel’s slope indicates it will soon exceed its profile”, with phase excursions only “when notching dips the control amplitude into the noise”.
  • Brinkman and Kretz (1992): on-line resonance detection to reduce the input automatically, at the design stage.

We found no published equation for how a multi-shaker sine controller shares a notch among its drives, and none for changing the reference’s amplitudes and relative phases to hold a limit with the least change to the test. Nor did we find a published law for a tracked resonance dwell with several shakers in environmental testing.

7. What Other Fields Know About Holding a Tone With Several Actuators

Holding a tone with several actuators is an old problem outside vibration testing. Active noise and vibration control cancels a tone at several microphones or sensors with several loudspeakers or actuators; helicopter higher harmonic control cancels the rotor’s tonal vibration in the cabin by pitching the blades; magnetic bearing controllers cancel a rotor’s unbalance response at the running speed. All three use the same structure as a sine test controller: a known frequency, a matrix of responses from the actuators to the sensors at that frequency, and an update of the actuator amplitudes and phases from the measured error, with the matrix known only approximately. The condition for such an update to converge when its matrix is wrong is printed in sources we read:

  • One channel, small gain, model identified as it runs. The update converges if and only if the phase difference between the model and the true response is less than 90 degrees: “the difference of phase between the estimated plant model and the true plant dynamics must be less than 90°”, and “the instability for phase difference greater than 90° is only local” (Pigg and Bodson 2011, Eq. 18; “plant” is their word for the system being controlled).
  • Several channels, small gain. Wu and Bodson (2004) give the averaged form for several channels: the eigenvalues of the true response matrix times the correction matrix must have positive real parts, which with one channel is the 90 degree condition.
  • Several channels, a full step. For the harmonic steady-state update of helicopter control, “the HSS algorithm is stable if and only if” the spectral radius of the correction matrix times the model error is below one (Chandrasekar and others 2004, Eq. 5.8).
  • One channel, a full step, by hand. Johnson (1982) states that the estimate “must have the same sign and at least 50% the magnitude of the true value”. He also describes, from earlier work by Shaw, a fixed-gain controller that became unstable when the response matrix changed with flight speed and was made stable by identifying the matrix as it flew.
  • Through a critical speed. Knospe and others (1994) carried a rotor’s unbalance control through two critical speeds, 2700 and 4200 rpm, in a 30 s run-up from 1500 to 5000 rpm, using gain matrices tabulated by speed and interpolated between them. A single gain designed at 2200 rpm went unstable above 2500 rpm, because “the T matrix changes rapidly with speed near the first critical”. Knospe and Tamer (1996) instead designed fixed gains to tolerate a range of speed, with design goals of 1.17 to 1.51 for the ratio of the vibration under the tolerant gain to that under the best gain, over ranges of plus or minus 20 to 45 percent; only the widest range spanned the first critical speed.

The classic papers on multichannel active control with an error in the model (Elliott, Boucher and Nelson 1992; Boucher, Elliott and Nelson 1991) were read as abstracts only, and we do not attribute the condition to them. The helicopter and rotor results matter here for a plain reason: a rotor run up through a critical speed is a sweep through a resonance, and the response matrix changes fastest where the mode is.

8. A Tone on a Structure That Changes With Level

Bolted joints, friction interfaces and thin members make a structure’s resonances move with the level of the test: down and with more damping as joints slip, up as a thin member stretches. The field of nonlinear vibration testing holds a tone on such a structure with feedback, and its methods are close to what a sine controller would need.

Phase-locked loops adjust the frequency until the phase between response and excitation equals a set value, which tracks a nonlinear resonance as the level changes. Hippold and others (2024) track a backbone curve with “only about 100 periods” per point, and note that earlier loop settings had been chosen “in a strictly heuristic way”; their tests use one shaker. In a companion method, Hippold and others (2025) remove the unwanted harmonics of the applied excitation with an integral loop on each harmonic; the loop’s stability sign “may change when Ω is varied around resonance”, and the remedy they give is a large enough proportional gain and a light exciter against the mode’s damping, chosen through the exciter and drive point. They state that their method is restricted to an imposed excitation frequency. It uses one exciter.

Control-based continuation follows a branch of periodic responses, stable or not, with a feedback controller. Sieber and Krauskopf (2008) introduced it on a simulated, autonomous dry-friction oscillator; it “does not require a mathematical model” and “relies on feedback stabilizability”. Abeloos and others (2021) made it stepped and swept with an adaptive filter, and show that a large enough derivative gain gives “a unique, stable” orbit.

Response-controlled stepped sine holds the response amplitude at the drive point constant, which “yields quasi-linear frequency response functions directly” (Karaağaçlı and Özgüven 2021). The authors state that it “can also be applied by using multi-point excitation”, in theory, and that near a turning point a “standard force-controller cannot avoid jumping from one stable branch to another”. A review of experimental continuation (Raze, Abeloos and Kerschen 2025) finds that the method “was not able to stabilize unstable” solutions, and notes that “an adaptation for multi-input-multi- output test case was sketched” in a 2020 conference paper (Tang, Stephan and Boeswald 2020), which we read as an abstract only: it extends a single-input phase-locked loop to several inputs by modal blending, to find the phase resonance of one mode. Peeters and others (2011) name “a more general constructive procedure for NNM force appropriation, resorting to several shakers with harmonics of the fundamental frequency” as future research. Peter and Leine (2017), Denis and others (2018) and Renson and others (2016) were read as abstracts only.

Worded to our search: every phase-locked loop, continuation and response-controlled experiment we read uses one exciter, and we found nothing that holds a specified amplitude at several points with several exciters through a nonlinear resonance.

How far a resonance may move under a low-level model (ours). Sections 3 and 7 meet here. Take one mode, its damping unchanged, whose resonance at test level sits δ\delta half-power bandwidths away from where the low-level model put it. Near a resonance the phase of the response turns through 180 degrees over a few bandwidths, following arctan\arctan of the distance from the resonance in half-bandwidths. The model’s phase and the structure’s phase then differ most midway between the two resonances, by

θmax=2arctan⁡(δ),\theta_{max} = 2 \arctan(\delta),

which is 53 degrees at half a bandwidth, 90 degrees at one bandwidth and 127 degrees at two. By the one-channel, small-gain condition of Pigg and Bodson, a correction through the low-level model stops converging near that midpoint once the resonance has moved about one half-power bandwidth, and the fixed-inverse law of Section 3.1 has no positive gain left there. We give this as the one-channel condition; we do not claim it as a general result for several channels, where the eigenvalues of Section 7 decide. One half-power bandwidth is a small move: 2 percent of the frequency for a QQ of 50 and 1 percent for a QQ of 100. For scale only, the ECSS standard accepts shifts under 5 percent between the resonance searches before and after a test (Table 1), which for a QQ of 50 is 2.5 bandwidths. The 1990 patent’s background speaks of negative gains beyond 90 degrees, and the patent adapts its model during the test.

9. Where the Published Work Stops

Each statement is worded to the searches in Section 10; a wider search may find what ours did not.

  1. Few laws, little evidence. Five published forms, four of which correct through a model measured once at a low level. The evidence is one two-axis table test that missed tolerance near the table’s own modes at 80 Hz, a two-shaker beam controlled in amplitude only, a cantilever beam under sine plus random, a simulation of two uncoupled shakers, and a run-to-run correction on two axes of a three-axis shaker. The one law that changes its model during the test has no published test of its printed equations.
  2. No published result on a structure whose resonances move with level under closed-loop multi-shaker sine control.
  3. The model along a sweep. No published law says how its model, gain or correction is carried from one frequency to the next in a continuous sweep, and no published analysis ties the speed of the correction to the sweep rate for several shakers.
  4. Control on a flexible article. One laboratory account (four small shakers under an aluminium plate, 5 to 80 Hz, figures only). No account of a qualification test controlled at points on a flexible article that reports tolerances through resonance or resonances that moved with level.
  5. Limits and notching. Published behaviour (scale the whole reference; notch early from a slope), and no equation for sharing a notch among several drives or reshaping the reference to hold a limit, for sine.
  6. Standards. No sine clause for several exciters and no tolerance on relative phase in the standards read.
  7. Sweep effects. Published for one mode and one exciter (ECSS, Davis and others, ISO 7626-2); nothing for several drives or for a resonance that moves with level.
  8. Nonlinear structures. Phase-locked loops, continuation and response control are published for one exciter; nothing found holds amplitude at several points with several exciters through a nonlinear resonance.
  9. Free relative phases. Amplitude-only references are used (Section 3.4), but no published law chooses the free phases, for example for the least drive or for continuity along the sweep.

The work from other fields is that of Bodson, Pigg and Wu; Chandrasekar, Liu, Patt, Friedmann and Bernstein; Johnson of NASA; Knospe and co-authors; and, in nonlinear testing, Hippold, Krack and co-authors, Sieber and Krauskopf, Abeloos, Raze, Kerschen and co-authors, Karaağaçlı and Özgüven, and Peeters, Kerschen and Golinval.
